# What roles do motor processes play in speech perception?

## The question

Speaking and listening recruit interacting neural and bodily systems. When someone hears a consonant, production-related regions may respond; disrupting some of those regions can alter certain listening tasks. The explanatory question is **what motor processes contribute**, rather than whether speech perception as a whole is intrinsically motoric.

Several answers remain viable. Production-related processes might generate predictions about forthcoming sound, help estimate articulatory causes, stabilise a weak phonetic interpretation, maintain or manipulate phonological information, or support decisions in demanding laboratory tasks. Their causal roles could depend on language experience, the quality of the acoustic signal, the judgement required and the particular cortical or bodily system involved. These hypotheses may coexist.

The available evidence supports **some specific, causal sensorimotor contributions to phonetic performance**, alongside substantial auditory routes to recognition and comprehension. It does not identify one obligatory motor operation used in all listening. The more difficult work is to specify the contribution in computational and representational terms and distinguish it from neighbouring processes.

The [parallel entry on gesture recovery](phonetic-gestures-and-speech-perception.md) asks what information about the *speaker's* articulatory actions the listener can extract. That is a distinct question. A listener's motor cortex might support a phoneme judgement without representing a speaker's gesture; conversely, articulatory-event information could be recovered through auditory or audiovisual pathways without mandatory motor simulation.

## Which behaviour are we explaining?

'Speech perception' covers tasks with different cognitive requirements. Discriminating whether two sounds differ, deciding which phoneme ended a word, understanding its meaning, and tracking a familiar speaker in noise should not be treated as interchangeable measures.

| Task or outcome | What successful performance directly shows | What it does not settle |
|---|---|---|
| Detecting a phonetic difference | Sensitivity to a change in the presented speech | Whether the listener explicitly labels it, or uses a motor representation |
| Identifying a phoneme | Assignment of a phonetic response/category | Whether earlier sensory representations discard within-category detail |
| Understanding a word or utterance | Access to lexical or semantic information | Whether explicit segment analysis or motor simulation was necessary |
| Recognising speech in noise | Robust performance under particular uncertainty | Which compensatory mechanisms were responsible |
| Decoding a neural feature during listening | A pattern covaries with a specified model variable | Whether that area is necessary, or what the experienced percept is of |

An experiment can show a genuine motor contribution while being silent about several of these outcomes. And 'motor cortex' covers disparate structures and functions: primary articulator maps, premotor regions, inferior frontal areas, somatosensory cortex and auditory–motor interface regions. A result about one cannot simply be assigned to all.

Three questions organise this entry: **Is there a causal contribution? What information or computation is involved? Under which conditions does it occur?** They require different forms of evidence.

## Causal effects: articulators, sensitivity and tasks

\citet{dausilio:2009_motor} applied transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS) over lip- and tongue-related motor representations and found articulator-specific facilitation or interference in speech-sound discrimination. The result supports more than the commonplace observation that speech listening coactivates production circuitry: manipulating a motor-related site changed performance in a way related to the articulator associated with the sound. The task was nevertheless an explicit laboratory discrimination, not ordinary comprehension, and stimulation effects do not by themselves determine the represented information.

A useful advance comes from \citet{smalle:2015_dissociating}. In their syllable discrimination task, disrupting the motor-lip representation temporarily reduced *sensitivity* to between-category changes, whereas disruption at a motor-hand site changed *response bias* rather than sensitivity. The distinction, made using signal detection analysis, matters greatly. A manipulation can change reports without changing the ability to hear a distinction; conversely, a measured sensitivity change gives stronger reason to posit a contribution to processing relevant speech information. Even then, 'perceptual sensitivity' in a task does not by itself settle whether the motor representation constitutes the perceptual content.

A particularly informative comparison holds the acoustic words relatively constant while changing the judgement. \citet{krieger-redwood:2013_selective} applied offline TMS to premotor cortex, posterior superior temporal gyrus (pSTG) or a visual control site. Premotor stimulation slowed explicit judgments about a word's final phoneme but left semantic category judgments for heard words unaffected. Stimulation of pSTG affected both linguistic tasks. This is a causal, site-sensitive dissociation: a role in deliberate phoneme analysis does not entail a corresponding necessity for mapping the same sound to meaning. The result concerns the tested responses and circumstances; a nonsignificant semantic effect does not show that premotor processing is never relevant to comprehension.

The relevance of articulatory systems begins before fluent speech. \citet{bruderer:2015_sensorimotor} constrained infants' tongue position with teething toys, checked the physical restriction using ultrasound, and reported impaired discrimination of a relevant non-native contrast in the selectively constrained condition. The finding points to an early sensorimotor influence without requiring prior skilled production of those sounds. It does not uniquely isolate motor commands: changed oral somatosensation, attention, and other bodily consequences of the constraint warrant consideration.

The more recent \citet{carneiropereira:2026_origin} compares silent opening/closing of the lips with lip protrusion/retraction during noisy syllable discrimination. Selective impairment for a labial-related contrast occurred with opening/closing, despite partial overlap in musculature with the other lip movement. This challenges an undifferentiated motor-resource-depletion account. It favours something specific about the *movement, gestural task or its predicted sensory consequences*. Because motor commands and bodily sensory feedback change together, the experiment does not yet choose uniquely among those explanations.

Taken together, intervention results make it unreasonable to deny **all** causal sensorimotor involvement. They also motivate a precise question: does the contribution depend on the *articulatory target*, on the *bodily state*, on a *predicted acoustic consequence*, or on the *kind of phonological decision*?

## What the dissociations show about necessity

The strongest historical motor theory treated production-related structure as obligatory for successful perception of speech. The clinical evidence weighs heavily against a simple version of that necessity claim.

\citet{rogalsky:2011_are} examined five patients selected for lesions involving parts of the purported human mirror system. Many receptive word-comprehension and discrimination measures were at or near ceiling despite substantial damage, whereas extension into temporal regions was associated with greater perceptual difficulty. The cases challenge a necessary role for the damaged frontoparietal structures in the tested receptive abilities. They do not amount to a selective deletion of every speech motor representation; lesions are heterogeneous and chronic reorganisation may matter.

The detailed single case of \citet{stasenko:2015_causal} makes a complementary distinction. Severe speech-production impairment after frontal damage coexisted with substantially preserved speech-sound *discrimination* and ordinary auditory comprehension, alongside difficulty in explicit phoneme labelling. This is especially informative about the gap between having access to an acoustic difference and making a category decision about it.

The positive lesion evidence from auditory structures must also be retained. \citet{binder:2025_lesion} links acoustic-phonetic discrimination deficits in a large left-hemisphere stroke sample to temporal auditory and adjacent regions, with distinctions across phonetic features. The evidence supports the importance of auditory pathways as active processors of phonetic structure. It leaves room for additional motor contributions and does not show that all relevant information is encoded exclusively in auditory form.

Clinical sparing defeats only the claims for which the relevant damaged structures are supposed to be **necessary for the preserved tasks**. It does not refute optional, compensatory, developmental or task-specific motor contributions. That more limited inference is already sufficient to redirect the research agenda.

## What information is present in production-related areas during listening?

Motor activation is a weak basis for inferring representational content. There are several importantly different tests in the newer literature.

**Auditory organisation within sensorimotor cortex.** Using intracranial recordings, \citet{cheung:2016_auditory} compared neural patterns elicited by speaking and listening. Production responses showed substantial articulatory organisation, whereas listening-related responses in ventral sensorimotor cortex were more organised along acoustic dimensions resembling auditory cortex. The detailed syllable analyses used a small clinically selected sample. The study demonstrates the risk of treating a response in 'motor cortex' as an articulatory motor code.

**Abstract syllable identity.** In fMRI representational similarity analyses, \citet{evans:2015_hierarchical} identified somatomotor response patterns that generalised across changes in speaker and acoustic transformation. This suggests representation of heard *syllable identity* relatively independent of surface acoustic form. Yet an invariant identity code could be phonological or learned audiomotor rather than a description of a particular gesture. The participants had been trained on degraded speech and performed an attentive one-back task. The authors explicitly distinguish activation from causal necessity.

**Generalisation of articulatory features.** \citet{correia:2015_decoding} trained fMRI classifiers on a place-of-articulation contrast in one manner and tested generalisation to another, with related contrasts for manner and voicing. Positive decoding extended into portions of premotor, motor and somatosensory regions as well as auditory cortex. This is more specifically relevant to articulatory structure than ordinary phoneme identity decoding, although residual regularities in acoustic consequences and an fMRI pattern's correlation with feature labels remain alternative interpretations.

**A failed passive-listening somatotopy test.** \citet{arsenault:2016_no} replicated and extended an earlier predicted lip-versus-tongue map using a movement localiser, univariate tests, multivariate classification and Bayesian analyses. The production localiser worked, but the corresponding place-of-articulation distinction was not demonstrable during passive listening under their conditions. The null finding is informative for an *obligatory* passive mapping, while sample size, stimuli and differences from the preceding positive studies constrain its scope.

These experiments need to be placed side by side rather than counted as votes for or against 'motor representation'. **Acoustic-feature coding**, **abstract phoneme identity**, **articulatory-feature geometry**, and **cross-task movement-to-listening correspondence** are different hypothesised properties. The cortical regions, statistical models, acoustic controls, participants and cognitive tasks differ.

The **unpublished bioRxiv preprint** \citet{tseng:2026_decoding} is of particular interest because its **unpublished, not-peer-reviewed** analysis trained a classifier on lip/tongue movements and tested classification of heard consonants. Reported above-chance transfer was confined to native plosives in noise in right precentral cortex. It provides a more targeted hypothesis about shared movement and listening representations, but the restriction of the finding and its **unpublished preprint** status preclude treating it as established replication or a general property of speech perception.

Finally, phonetic-feature information is also found outside the production network: intracranial superior-temporal data in \citet{mesgarani:2014_phonetic} show rich phonetic organisation. Region-specific evidence must be compared with strong auditory representations and, where possible, acoustic-feature models. The comparison in \citet{daube:2019_simple} shows why: some apparent high-level phoneme decodability in continuous-speech MEG/EEG can be explained by appropriate acoustic-edge predictors. Decoding a feature label should not be equated with having identified the neural computation that derives it.

## Noise, difficulty and compensation

One attractive proposal is that listeners recruit internal production models when acoustics are degraded. It predicts specific changes with uncertainty, but 'more motor activity in noise' is too coarse a formulation.

\citet{du:2014_noise} reports that patterns in left ventral premotor and Broca-related regions discriminated a small set of syllable categories at noise levels where the tested auditory ROI patterns showed reduced phoneme discriminability. Its fMRI results motivate a compensatory interpretation. Yet listeners performed explicit choices among four syllables, and decoding resilience alone does not show motor-to-auditory compensation, causal rescue or greater accuracy attributable to the motor region.

A different result comes from \citet{perron:2024_left}. MEG during syllable discrimination indicated left ventral primary motor and bilateral posterior superior temporal responses sensitive to phonological difference, but increasing noise did **not** produce the straightforward increase in frontal motor contribution predicted by a simple compensation account. The methods, neural measures and stimulus regime differ from the fMRI study; neither should be read as a direct failed replication of the other.

The wider quantitative picture is also anatomically selective. A coordinate-based meta-analysis by \citet{perron:2024_engagement}, combining 89 challenging-speech perception studies and 35 speech-production studies, reported perception–production overlap particularly around frontal operculum and planum temporale, with context-dependent pre-SMA/inferior-frontal involvement. It also explicitly discusses the absence of primary motor or premotor overlap under the applied meta-analytic thresholds. Speech in noise, spectrally degraded speech and linguistic complexity show different patterns of demands. Activation overlap identifies neither identical information codes nor causal influence.

Thus the empirically useful noise question is **which subregion contributes which representation or operation, at which degree and kind of uncertainty, while the listener is doing what?** Difficulty may recruit attention, executive selection or memory in addition to—and sometimes instead of—gesture-related predictions.

## Candidate functions and discriminating tests

The evidence motivates several candidate mechanisms. They should be presented as hypotheses with different consequences.

### Auditory prediction using production knowledge

An internal account of how articulatory movements generate sound could predict the next acoustic features or help infer a likely cause from impoverished input. This might be useful during fast, accented or noisy speech. To support the claim, one would want more than premotor activation: a perturbation of the specific hypothesised predictor should selectively reduce the *benefit of informative production expectations*, and the effect should survive matched acoustic uncertainty and decision demands. It would also matter whether the computed variable is a gesture, a phonetic label or a predicted sound. The [gesture-recovery inquiry](phonetic-gestures-and-speech-perception.md) develops this difference.

### Access to and manipulation of phoneme categories

A motor-related process might assist explicit segment identification, rehearsal or comparison without being obligatory for the more direct route from words to meaning. \citet{krieger-redwood:2013_selective} makes this hypothesis especially plausible. Within-participant tasks that compare explicit phoneme decisions, implicit discrimination and semantic responses to the **same** acoustic items would help isolate this function, particularly when phonological working-memory and response requirements are separately manipulated.

### Movement-specific sensorimotor coupling

The effects of constraining a mouth or silently executing a particular movement suggest interactions between auditory processing, motor tasks and somatosensory estimates \citep{bruderer:2015_sensorimotor,carneiropereira:2026_origin}. A candidate mechanism might be an articulator-specific prediction or body-state estimate. To discriminate it from generic motor competition, an experiment should contrast **different movements using the same articulators**, manipulate somatosensory feedback independently where feasible, and verify the movements rather than infer them from instructions.

### Decisions, strategies and executive control

A listener might recruit frontal and motor-adjacent systems to maintain categories, choose a response, or increase effort under uncertainty. Such activity can be useful while leaving the underlying acoustic-phonetic representation largely unchanged. The signal-detection contrast in \citet{smalle:2015_dissociating} makes a concrete distinction between changes in perceptual sensitivity and response criterion; the task-dependent dissociation in \citet{krieger-redwood:2013_selective} provides another. These are serious mechanistic alternatives, rather than generic ways of dismissing positive motor results.

A good research programme should let these processes **coexist** where the evidence requires it. It should still ask which is necessary for a *particular outcome*, which is compensatory, and which merely accompanies a task.

## What would make the evidence more decisive?

A strong programme would integrate causal and representational methods in designs whose alternatives predict different results. Four priorities stand out.

**Cross tasks within the same stimuli and listeners.** Compare implicit change detection, explicit phoneme decisions and semantic understanding, with response format, speed–accuracy tradeoffs, phonological memory and difficulty controlled. Report sensitivity and bias separately where appropriate. A selective effect on explicit judgments would support a different role from a selective effect on rapid, implicit discrimination.

**Model the competing information.** Test acoustic-feature, phonological-identity, articulatory-feature and production-localiser representational models on the same neural data. Cross-speaker and cross-manner generalisation help, but do not automatically remove acoustic covariation. A convincing production-format result should outperform well-designed acoustic and categorical alternatives on held-out material.

**Combine intervention with measurement.** If a neural pattern is proposed to encode an articulatory variable necessary for perceptual recovery, ask whether selective interference changes the listener's sensitivity to that variable rather than merely the response. Temporal and anatomical specificity, control-site stimulation and independent movement recordings would narrow interpretation; none alone proves the contents of experience.

**Probe uncertainty and learning as separate dimensions.** Vary signal-to-noise ratio, unfamiliar accent or new contrast, and task demands independently. If motor-based prediction helps when evidence is insufficient, the size and direction of its contribution should follow a principled measure of uncertainty and prior articulatory knowledge. An indiscriminate increase with task difficulty would fit executive-control accounts equally well.

## Assessment and connections

Motor contributions to speech-related behaviour are real, heterogeneous and sometimes articulator-specific. The best intervention evidence supports selective influences on phonetic sensitivity or categorisation, while clinical and task dissociations weigh against an obligatory motor route for ordinary recognition. Neural evidence concerns multiple kinds of represented information, and its apparent disagreements partly arise from different hypotheses and experimental contexts.

A useful overall hypothesis is that **production-related resources supply optional, context-dependent information or computations to an auditory–linguistic system**. But 'prediction', 'simulation' and 'sensorimotor integration' are still programme labels unless we specify the variables represented, the operation performed and the conditions of causal relevance. This is what further research should seek to determine.

The connection to [gesture recovery](phonetic-gestures-and-speech-perception.md) is a particularly promising candidate: motor knowledge might help estimate the articulatory causes or goals behind a heard signal. That hypothesis neither follows from observed motor effects nor entails that gestures are perceptual objects. Whether experience of speech actions contributes to the concepts used in intentional speech control is pursued separately in [speech actions and the intention–motor interface](speech-actions-interface-problem.md).

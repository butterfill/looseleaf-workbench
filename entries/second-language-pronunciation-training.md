# How do second-language learners acquire new phonetic distinctions, and what might this reveal about learning speech actions?

## The phenomenon to explain

An adult whose first language lacks a particular phonetic contrast can learn to distinguish it and, sometimes, to produce it. The interesting question is **how an initially unavailable distinction becomes something the learner can hear and do**. There is a possible further question: can acquiring a way of acting change which *kinds of speech action* the learner can experience or deliberately intend?

We must distinguish several achievements:

1. **Auditory sensitivity:** hearing a difference between two tokens, perhaps without identifying either as an instance of a particular phoneme.
2. **Phonetic categorisation:** identifying the contrast across new words, voices and contexts, rather than remembering individual examples.
3. **Production:** making utterances that listeners classify as instances of the intended contrast.
4. **Flexible use:** preserving the contrast across rates, phonetic surroundings, novel words and meaning-focused speech, rather than only in practised repetitions.
5. **Deliberate articulatory control:** selecting, sustaining or changing a way of producing the contrast when there are multiple possible realisations.
6. **Action understanding:** perceiving or thinking about a relevant *articulatory event as an action*, rather than merely classifying an acoustic signal.

These are related capacities, but one is no proxy for another. In particular, a *phoneme or auditory category* is not automatically an *articulatory gesture*. A new L2 contrast may involve newly coordinating and timing movements the learner can already perform; producing a recognisable sound need not reveal which vocal-tract task the learner represents motorically. The distinction matters for the proposed connection to the Interface Problem.

## How learners acquire a new perceptual distinction

**They learn against the background of L1 categories.** An unfamiliar L2 sound may initially be assimilated to a familiar L1 category; cues that matter in the target language may be underweighted, or two L2 sounds may be treated as variants of one category. The speech-learning and perceptual-assimilation traditions offer accounts of these starting difficulties. Learning can involve attending differently to spectral, temporal or contextual cues and establishing more reliable distinctions among tokens. These are plausible mechanisms, not mechanisms uniquely identified by the meta-analyses below.

**Varied examples plus corrective feedback work.** In high-variability phonetic training (HVPT), learners typically identify or discriminate examples from multiple speakers in varied words or phonetic contexts, receiving feedback on their choices. The synthesis by \citet{uchihara:2025_high} gives substantial evidence that this improves perceptual performance. Improvement often persists beyond the immediate test and extends, with some loss, to unfamiliar items and talkers. That matters: learners can gain something more general than a memory for a few recorded sounds.

A natural explanation is the development or retuning of phonetic categories through accumulated examples and attention to diagnostic cues. But a successful identification task alone cannot show whether the change is in sensory discriminability, cue weighting, category representations, learned label–sound associations, or some combination. Identification training and identification testing can also favour the same acquired task skill. The HVPT findings support perceptual *learning* much more securely than a unique theory of *what is represented*.

Nor do they isolate the separate causal contributions of multiple voices, varied contexts and feedback: canonical HVPT bundles these together. It would be premature to infer either that more talkers always help or that the learner identifies the speaker's motor gesture.

## How hearing a contrast relates to producing it

**The connection is real but loose.** Perception-focused training sometimes improves production even when students have not explicitly practised speaking during training. This is one reason to think that speech perception and production are connected rather than independent. Yet \citet{uchihara:2024_does} found that the production benefits of perceptual HVPT are less secure than its perceptual benefits: improvements were clearer for trained items than for new items, and convincing evidence of durable production transfer was limited. Across learners, improvements in perception and production were scarcely correlated, even though studies with larger perceptual gains tended to have larger production gains. A correlation between study averages cannot establish that perceptual change in a given learner *caused* their articulatory change.

The original Speech Learning Model gives perceptual categories a leading role in later production. Its revision (SLM-r) allows reciprocal development between perceptual and productive systems. Both are compatible with the broad evidence, as is a picture in which the systems are distinct but coupled. The studies do not warrant either the claim that accurate perception automatically supplies an executable motor plan or the claim that knowing how to articulate the sound is necessary for perceptual discrimination.

An important asymmetry is easy to miss: hearing the relevant distinction gives a learner a better **target** for an utterance, but attaining that target requires learning how to coordinate the vocal tract. Production also supplies auditory and somatosensory feedback which may alter subsequent performance and perception. Work on experienced speakers illustrates this interaction: auditory-feedback adaptation can affect later vowel categorisation \citep{lametti:2014_plasticity}; phonetic categories can constrain feedback compensation \citep{niziolek:2013_vowel}; and lexical context can influence adaptation \citep{bourguignon:2014_lexicalperceptual}. These are demonstrations of sensorimotor plasticity, not experiments comparing methods of L2 instruction.

## How learners gain productive control

Production training invites learners to imitate, practise and adjust articulations. Explicit teaching may direct attention to tongue placement, constriction, voicing, aspiration or timing; teachers may use diagrams, ultrasound or acoustic visual feedback. Such instruction could help a learner discover a vocal-tract strategy and calibrate it against auditory and bodily consequences. Repetition alone, however, does not show what the learner has learned to represent or deliberately select.

There is a relevant hint in \citet{uchihara:2024_does}: perceptual-training studies which also gave **explicit phonetic information** tended to show stronger gains in production. This is a cross-study moderator, not a controlled demonstration that articulatory explanation produces a new motor representation. Nor does it show that production practice should always begin immediately. The authors discuss the possibility that premature, inaccurately self-produced input could impede perceptual calibration, but their review does not directly compare schedules of production practice.

The broader synthesis by \citet{yao:2025_metaanalysis} includes perceptual, productive and combined training. It finds improvement in both perceptual and productive outcomes, with effects strongly dependent on training and test tasks. Its apparent ranking of training approaches is based on heterogeneous studies, not on a clean, matched comparison of ways to acquire a gesture. In particular, its low mean estimate for combined training is **not** evidence that combining perception and production is intrinsically counterproductive. The most useful lesson is the need to determine which *capacity* a particular intervention changes, under which conditions.

## A new capacity on request is different from fluent use

\citet{saito:2019_effects} shows why the choice of outcome measure is decisive. Explicit pronunciation instruction most clearly improves **specific features in monitored, controlled production**, for example a trained vowel in a reading task. Its effects on spontaneous, meaning-focused pronunciation and on global judgments of comprehensibility or intelligibility are much less certain. The review distinguishes the taught feature, the method of scoring it, and the degree of control allowed by the test.

This suggests a possible learning trajectory: noticing a distinction, learning to attain a target while monitoring one's performance, then proceduralising and automating its use amid other demands. But the review does not establish that this is a universal sequence, or distinguish all the learning processes involved.

Two philosophical cautions follow. Spontaneous use is evidence of integration into a working speech skill, **not necessarily an act of explicitly choosing a phonetic gesture**. Conversely, carefully pronouncing a word when prompted shows some controllability but need not show an intention whose content specifies the bodily speech action, as opposed to a phonological target or a remembered utterance. To investigate deliberate control, we would need to test flexible selection and modification of articulatory outcomes independently of successful repetition and phoneme labelling.

## Possible relevance to the Interface Problem

The proposal in \citet{sinigaglia:2015_puzzle} is that motor representations can shape experiences revelatory of action, thereby supplying content-respecting reasons for thoughts about actions despite the inferential isolation of motor representation from thought. The further possibility that the *ability to think about certain action types* depends on abilities to represent them motorically is speculative. L2 learning offers an unusually promising case in which a learner's perceptual and motor repertoires can be studied **before and after** acquisition.

There are two separable lines of inquiry.

**Learning to match an intended outcome with a motor outcome.** A learner may start with the intention to say a particular word or make a sound distinction without being able to implement it reliably. Acquiring the skill may involve learning which motor plans achieve the intended phonological result in changing contexts. This would be evidence about how reliable intention–motor matching is *acquired*. It would not, on its own, tell us how thoughts have the requisite format or how matching is selected among alternative motor solutions. Nor should a phonological plan be assumed to be an intention.

**Learning to experience and think about an action type.** A more distinctive prediction inspired by Sinigaglia and Butterfill would be that acquiring a new repertoire of articulatory actions changes how a learner perceptually *organises* articulatory events: perhaps they can now experience or track a particular kind of constriction or coordinated vocal-tract action as such, and thereby acquire new reasons for thinking about and selecting it. A change in forced-choice phoneme identification does not test this prediction. Perceptual categories may be acquired without equivalent production skill; the category represented in such tests need not be a gesture at all. This is an opportunity to investigate a dependence claim, not evidence for it already.

The two lines might converge, but alternative explanations remain available: learned sound–label associations, better sensory targets, new motor schemas, and learned translations between phonological and motor representations could each explain substantial behavioural progress without positing motor-shaped experience. These mechanisms may also cooperate rather than compete.

## A more discriminating research question

Can learners who are **equally good at identifying a novel contrast** differ in their ability to intentionally deploy a particular articulatory distinction and in the *action structure* they experience when hearing or observing it? A useful longitudinal comparison would contrast perceptual HVPT with training adding targeted articulatory guidance and feedback, controlling exposure, time and test familiarity as far as possible.

Measure separately: (a) discrimination and identification with unfamiliar talkers and words; (b) **actual articulation**, using acoustic measures together with articulatory evidence where feasible; (c) production and intentional switching under changed rates, contexts, feedback and new communicative demands; (d) retention and spontaneous use; and (e) gesture- or event-sensitive experience and judgments that cannot be explained merely by phoneme labels or acoustic discriminability. Some acoustic outcomes can be achieved by more than one bodily realisation, making the articulatory measure especially important. Even a link between motor learning and changed event experience would need controls and interventions before being interpreted as *experience mediating thought*.

**Current answer:** adult L2 learners demonstrably acquire new perceptual distinctions and can improve their production. The most secure account is of interacting but partly dissociable perceptual category learning and sensorimotor skill learning, with generalisation and automaticity as further achievements. The evidence does not yet show that a newly acquired phonetic category is a newly available *action concept*, or that motor representation shapes the experience through which such a concept becomes available. That precise gap makes this literature useful for developing the philosophical conjecture.

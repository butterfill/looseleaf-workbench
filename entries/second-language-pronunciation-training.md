# How is second-language pronunciation taught, and what might it reveal about learning to intend speech actions?

## The question

Adults can learn to hear and produce phonetic contrasts absent from their first language. What kinds of teaching support that achievement? More philosophically, can instruction reveal whether gaining a new articulatory skill also makes a *new kind of speech action* available for deliberate choice? The educational literature is suggestive, but it rarely measures that last capacity directly.

## Distinguish the teaching goals

A pronunciation teacher may seek **intelligibility** (being understood), **comprehensibility** (how easy speech is to understand), improved **phonetic accuracy** on a target contrast, **fluency**, or approximation to a native-speaker accent. These outcomes need not covary. Classroom practice therefore cannot be described simply as making students pronounce sounds “correctly”. A training result on isolated syllables need not generalise to conversation, and improved perception need not imply voluntary control of tongue and lip configurations.

## What teachers and researchers do

**Perception-focused practice** presents examples of a contrast, often from several talkers and in several phonetic contexts, and asks learners to identify or discriminate them with corrective feedback. *High-variability phonetic training* aims to prevent learners from memorising a single voice or token and to encourage generalisable categories. A learner might distinguish English /r/ and /l/ across words and speakers without yet reliably producing the contrast.

**Production practice** elicits target sounds, syllables, words and connected utterances, using repetition, imitation, recasts and corrective feedback. Instruction may give explicit cues about tongue position, voicing, aspiration, lip rounding or stress; some approaches use diagrams, mirrors, ultrasound tongue imaging, acoustic spectrograms or real-time formant displays. Such cues make otherwise hidden articulatory properties more accessible, but learners may improve through imitation or auditory calibration without forming explicit concepts of every gesture.

**Communicative and prosodic practice** embeds pronunciation in intelligible speech: word stress, rhythm, prominence and intonation can matter more to successful communication than eliminating every segmental accent. A good assessment separates trained-token accuracy from intelligibility for unfamiliar listeners and from transfer to novel words or conversational settings.

These are families of techniques, not a single standard curriculum. Their relative effectiveness depends on learners' first language, target contrast, experience, teaching time, feedback, testing method and learning goal. This note is a preliminary map of instructional options, **not** a verified comparative meta-analysis.

## What follows from the existing speech-learning evidence?

\citet{lametti:2014_plasticity} shows that adapting production to altered auditory feedback can change subsequent vowel categorisation. It does **not** show that articulatory instruction is superior to perceptual training in a classroom, or that a new action concept has been acquired. \citet{niziolek:2013_vowel} shows that a vowel-category boundary can influence compensation, suggesting that learned categories may constrain online control. \citet{bourguignon:2014_lexicalperceptual} finds lexical-context sensitivity of adaptation. All three concern experienced speakers under controlled laboratory conditions, not direct comparisons of L2 teaching methods. For the production/perception distinction and its limitations, see [the companion speech-perception inquiry](phonetic-gestures-and-speech-perception.md).

## A philosophically discriminating conjecture

Suppose two groups reach similar identification accuracy for a novel contrast. One group has also received articulatory guidance and practised producing the contrast under variable conditions; the other has mainly heard and classified exemplars. A possible prediction is that the former group will more readily **deliberately select, preserve and modify** the contrast in new contexts. But this would not yet discriminate an *experience-mediated action-concept* account from ordinary motor practice, schema learning, strengthened associations or improved perceptual targets.

A stronger test would independently measure (i) identification/discrimination, (ii) production accuracy and generalisation, (iii) deliberate instruction-sensitive control over a contrast under novel perturbations, and (iv) whether the learner groups perceived speech into different *events* rather than merely choosing different labels. Pre/post testing, active controls and delayed retention are essential. Even a dissociation among these measures would require a causal argument before inferring that motor representation *structures thought*.

A feasible first step is therefore **not** to demand a decisive experiment on the Interface Problem, but to compare existing training designs and identify which already measure anything beyond accuracy and discrimination. The stronger philosophical experiment remains a research programme.

## Evidence gaps

There is substantial L2-pronunciation pedagogy and phonetic-training literature still to review before making comparative effectiveness claims. In particular, a systematic search must identify recent meta-analyses of pronunciation instruction, high-variability phonetic training, explicit articulatory teaching and visual feedback; audit their original full texts; and separate effects on perception, production, retention, intelligibility and transfer. None of these outcomes should be assigned invented effect sizes. The source checklist records the limits of the current access.

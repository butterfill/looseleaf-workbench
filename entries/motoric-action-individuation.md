## Hypothesis and distinctions

The motor system may partly structure **which portions of unfolding bodily activity are available as individual actions in thought**. Three claims must be distinguished: {==(1) motor representations facilitate recognizing already individuated actions; (2) they influence perceived temporal boundaries in an activity; and (3) they help determine the *privileged units*, and perhaps the nested part–whole structure, in terms of which activity is thought about==}{##328ad052-dd1a-4014-92fa-6460158462a2##}. The target is (3). Evidence for (1) does not entail (2); evidence for (2) does not entail (3).

The second half of a reach together with the first half of a grasp can certainly be *described* as a temporal part of behavior. The suggestion is that it is not normally *one motorically coherent action*: reaching and grasping are units organized around outcomes, whereas that cross-boundary stretch is not. This would at most establish psychologically privileged units, not a metaphysical impossibility of treating arbitrary stretches as actions. The units might also change with training and purposes.

The conclusion of \citet{sinigaglia:2015_puzzle} explicitly proposes that capacities to think about certain action types might depend on capacities to represent them motorically, and sketches an analogy with **object indexes**. One development is an *action index*: motoric representations may support tracking *this unfolding action* through its changing bodily configurations, expected outcome and nesting within a larger action. Indexing would not on its own determine every concept applied to an action. The 2015 paper offers an experience-mediated way for motor representations to have content-respecting effects on thought; whether the present proposed individuation effect uses that interface is unresolved. \citet{sinigaglia:2020_motor} already covers the skill-to-observational-knowledge route, but individuation is a distinct question.

## Findings: event segmentation and action descriptions

\citet{zacks:2001_perceiving} reports that observers identify coarse and fine units in videos of ordinary activity, with hierarchical alignment between boundary locations; their descriptions and memory exhibit related partonomic organization. This matters because descriptions concern the units of action *thought*, not merely button presses marking perceptual discontinuities. **Important negative evidence:** in that study's Experiments 2 and 3, laboratory training and expertise did not consistently improve hierarchical boundary alignment or yield markedly different segmentation distributions. The familiarization/expertise findings must not be described as unequivocal support for the motoric proposal.

\citet{zacks:2004_using} finds that changes in motion features of animations predict perceived event boundaries, with modulation by intentional organization. \citet{Baldwin:2008mw} demonstrates that statistical learning of sequential structure can produce action segmentation even with constraints on linguistic recoding. Both provide alternatives to explaining all action segmentation through motor representations. Importantly, movement features in stimuli are not the observer's *motor* representations.

Su and Swallow (2024) report that observers detect changes of action and changes of goal in everyday behavior that cannot be reduced simply to visual movement and object-contact changes. These distinctions make the target more specific, but do not establish a motor origin. Sun and McDougle (2026) report perceptual boundary effects **within** very brief actions: even a recognizably single act may have internal event structure. Thus a segmentation boundary need not distinguish two actions.

## Findings: skill and motor organization

Bläsing (2015) found differences between dancers and novices in segmentation of dance, plus changes in some trials after eight amateur dancers learned a dance phrase over approximately six weeks. The before/after effect was **not uniform across trials**, and physical training also changes observation, expectations and conceptual knowledge; no decisive isolation of motor representation follows.

Levine et al. (2017) report that segmentation of figure skating aligns with intentional structure, and that expertise—unlike mere visual familiarization—improved alignment. Stadler et al. (2021) found that taekwondo experts agreed more than novices about **where boundaries occur**, even for less familiar styles; the groups did **not simply differ in number of segments**. These are intriguing because the hypothesis concerns preferred units, not just coarser/finer parsing. Shared conceptual knowledge of techniques remains an alternative. Di Nota et al. (2020) report genre-specific dance-expertise effects and an associated dance-word recall result; the latter is not direct evidence for individuation.

Schack and Mechsner (2006) describe hierarchies of *basic action concepts* in skilled tennis serves. This may offer an independent measure of privileged functional units, but their long-term-memory representations cannot automatically be equated with online motor representations. Frank, Land and Schack (2016) found clearer changes in measured representational organization with combined physical and mental practice than with physical practice alone, suggesting that motor performance improvement and cognitive structural change may dissociate.

At the neuronal level \citet{bonini:2011_grasping} reports distinct coding of constituent grasping acts and larger eating/placing action goals in monkey parietal/premotor areas during complex action sequences, including early goal-selective responses sensitive to remembered information. This supports hierarchically organised candidate motor units. It does **not** show that those units determine human thought about action individuals.

Pomp et al. (2024) find that contact and release between hands and objects, and associations involving ordinary objects, structure segmentation. Contact dynamics and object knowledge may explain boundaries without invoking motor representation as a constitutive organizer.

## Rival direction of explanation

\citet{pomiechowska:2017_motor} found EEG mu-suppression during observation depended on whether an observed act was construed as instrumental rather than communicative. One possible explanation is that **prior interpretation recruits motor activation**, instead of motor activation generating that interpretation. Mu suppression is only an indirect, contested motor marker; the result is not a general disproof of motor influences. But it prevents treating motor activation during action observation as decisive evidence of motoric structuring.

## Uncertainties

- Segmentation, motor chunking, action recognition, goal inference, descriptive units and individuation in thought are not equivalent outcomes.
- Expertise co-varies with motor ability, visual familiarity, attention, task understanding, language, learned conventions and knowledge of goals.
- Motor chunking might cause action segmentation, or both might be effects of goal-based conceptual structuring; motor resonance might follow interpretation.
- Action structure is hierarchical and scale-dependent; a theory must explain why arbitrary cross-boundary segments are disfavored without making them unthinkable or treating every internal boundary as a new action.
- It is open whether an influence is necessary, causal but defeasible, or merely facilitative, and whether it operates through action experience.
- Several of the newest or most relevant sources have not received original-full-text checks. The search is not systematic, and small experiments and less selective publication venues demand caution.

## A discriminating study

Train two groups to perform the **same visible continuous sequence** of six movements while inducing different independently measurable motor chunks (e.g. **1–3 | 4–6** versus **1–2 | 3–4 | 5–6**). Verify the *motor* partition using initiation latency, transition costs, interference or transfer, not self-reported segmentation. Match visual experience, movement trajectories, semantic instruction and overall goal; include visual-only and explicit conceptual-grouping control conditions.

Then have both groups watch the **identical recording** of a new performer and assess not just boundary presses but judgements about which portions constitute *one action*, how parts are described and remembered, whether action identity is maintained through occlusion or interruption, and whether the effects generalize. The strong result would be a motor-organization-specific shift in what constitutes an individual action *in thought* over and above familiarity and explicit knowledge. Mere faster recognition or different segment counts would not suffice. A separate intervention disrupting specific motor representations could strengthen the causal case.

## Next research steps

Look for training or impairment studies independently measuring motor chunks *and* action-individuation judgements; search motor-learning, event segmentation, developmental, apraxia and perturbation literatures for disconfirming as well as confirming studies. Read and check external papers closely, especially null effects; assess source selection and evidential quality before revising the main thesis. This inquiry is at workbench stage, not ready to be promoted.

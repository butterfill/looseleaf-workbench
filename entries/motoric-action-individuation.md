# Could motor organization help determine what counts as an action in thought?

*Looseleaf workbench research entry — 10 October 2026, revised after citation and original-source audit. Preliminary: a selective critical investigation, not a systematic review or a settled argument for a motoric theory of action individuation.*

## The original question

> I know there's a bit of research on motor processes being involved in thinking generally. I was never super impressed by that research, somehow neither the theory nor the findings seemed so compelling to me. A more promising idea—it seems to me—is that motor processes might be involved in thinking about actions.
>
> It's important to try to be precise about the idea I'm after. The basic mirroring phenomena might show that there is a facilitation effect in recognizing others' actions. But that could be explained without any very close connection between the motor processes and the thinking.
>
> What I'm after is rather the idea that the motoric plays a role in structuring thought about action. I don't yet have a good analogy. But my sense is that which things count as actions (reaching does, and grasping does but the second part of a reaching action and the first part of a grasping action would not) depends in some way on how things are represented motorically.
>
> Please check my \citet{sinigaglia:2015_puzzle} for background.
>
> What research have I not already considered that could help here?

## The hypothesis should be about individuation, not facilitation

The key difference is between three claims:

1. **Facilitation of recognition:** representing an observed action motorically makes it easier or faster to identify it as reaching, grasping, drinking, and so on. This is not the target.
2. **Segmentation of activity:** motor processes partly determine which *temporal boundaries* an observer detects in an unfolding activity.
3. **Individuation of actions in thought:** motor organization makes certain temporally extended portions of activity, rather than others, available as *one action* that can be represented, identified across occasions, tracked while unfolding, placed in a part–whole hierarchy, and used in inferences.

The motivating conjecture concerns **(3)**. Evidence for (2) can be informative but is not sufficient. A motor system may have multiple nested control structures, event perception may mark transitions *within* a single action, and a participant can mark an event boundary without having decided that two distinct actions occurred. Equally, the second half of reaching followed by the first half of grasping is perfectly *describable* as a temporal portion of activity: any claim that it is literally unthinkable is too strong. What requires explanation is why a *reach* and a *grasp* have a privileged status as units while the artificially joined cross-boundary stretch typically does not.

The strongest initially defensible proposal is **selective structuring** rather than absolute necessity: the motoric may make certain units more accessible as individual actions, holding perceptual evidence and explicit goal knowledge constant. A stronger dependence or constitutive claim should be kept distinct until there is intervention evidence.

This develops an open possibility in \citet{sinigaglia:2015_puzzle}. That paper argues that motor representations, despite being inferentially isolated from beliefs and intentions, sometimes have *content-respecting* effects on thoughts about goals via experiences of action; its conclusion conjectures that the ability to think about some action types may depend on motor representational abilities. Its comparison with perceptual object indexes suggests a useful **action-index** analogy: perhaps outcome-directed motor organization partly sustains experience or tracking of *this action* through its changing bodily stages. Such an index would help fix a unit for thought without itself supplying all conceptual descriptions of it. \citet{sinigaglia:2020_motor} further explores skill and observational knowledge, but the present question is sharper than enhanced knowledge from observation.

## What the event-segmentation literature actually establishes

### Hierarchical event representations can structure descriptions and learning

\citet{Zacks:2001vo} is an appropriate starting point because it links people's segmentation of everyday activity at coarse and fine grains with **how they describe and remember those parts**. Boundaries at different grains tend to align, and descriptions suggest nested partonomies. But the result must be reported alongside **Experiments 2–3**, where training and expertise **did not produce the predicted increase in hierarchical organization**. One training statistic went against the prediction and another was null; boundary patterns were not reliably different between trained and untrained or between experts and novices. This is a real constraint on a straightforward “motor learning restructures perceived action” story.

A historical candidate for connecting observed action structure with later performance is \citet{Hard:2006qm}, which proposed an **enclosure** measure (coarse unit boundaries following related fine ones) and claimed associations between hierarchical perceptual encoding and subsequent assembly. **However, this article was formally RETRACTED in 2008. Its authors said the research results could not be relied upon (Hard, Lozano & Tversky, 2008, retraction notice DOI [10.1037/a0013753](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1037/a0013753)). Its reported experiments and statistics must not be treated as evidence.** The difference between *coincident boundaries* and *genuinely nested action parts* remains a useful **conceptual and methodological question**, but it needs independent empirical validation. The retracted article should not be invoked to establish either motor-to-thought or thought-to-motor causation.

\citet{Kurby:2008bk} integrates the perception, memory, hierarchy and prediction literature. \citet{su:2024_people} takes a useful step toward discriminating kinds of unit: untrained adults viewing everyday activity marked **action changes** much more frequently than **goal changes** (about 18 vs 4 marks per minute), and their judgements predicted event boundaries beyond a global visual-change index and object-touch transitions. This helps specify the explanandum as action-related structure, not generic visual discontinuity. But the instructions explicitly distinguished action from goal and no motor process was intervened on.

Most importantly, \citet{sun:2026_perceiving} shows consistent perceptual boundaries **inside very brief, apparently “atomic” actions**. A deliberately chosen internal boundary (Experiment 1) predicted reduced sensitivity to incidental temporal and spatial visual changes there in later experiments; semantic manipulations suggested more than trivial pixel-level change. Yet the *whole* kick, swing or jump can still be one action. Hence **a perceptual event boundary must not be identified with a boundary between action individuals**. Even an implicit detection deficit at a boundary cannot, by itself, answer the original question.

### Non-motor segmentation mechanisms are substantial rivals

\citet{Zacks:2004vd} shows that movement properties of observed animations reliably predict perceived event changes, interacting with interpretations of intentional structure. \citet{Baldwin:2008mw} establishes that people can discover boundaries through statistical regularities in streams of human movements, even under constraints on linguistic recoding. Both show ways to carve activity without importing an observer's own motor repertoire as the source of the units.

\citet{reynolds:2007_computational} deserves special attention. Connectionist networks learned to predict successive states of continuous movement sequences; shifts in prediction error served to gate event-model updates. In one self-organizing simulation the network detected most of the **experimenter-designated transitions** (approximately 83%, with about 11% false alarms). It offers a concrete **computational alternative** for the emergence of event boundaries. But its input comprised *preconstructed sequences concatenated at experimenter-defined joins*; success at these joins does not tell us which portions are *actions* in the original sense, still less settle whether humans' action concepts depend on motor control. This distinction keeps the computational result in its evidential place.

A further concrete alternative is the influence of object relations: \citet{pomp:2024_action} shows that touching and untouching of manipulable items structure event-boundary judgements and neural activity. Replacing ordinary manipulable objects by dough pieces (in **different studies**, not one randomized manipulation) changed processing; in some respects dough sequences were segmented **more consistently**, not less. Object identity, learned affordances, and contact relations remain possible explanations for boundaries. The between-study and object/dough differences caution against a motor-specific inference.

## Where motor organization might supply candidate units

\citet{bonini:2011_grasping} is neurophysiologically close to the proposed motor side: in macaques executing complex sequences, parietal and premotor neurons differentiated constituent **grasping acts** from the wider eating/placing goal in which each grasp was embedded. Certain early parietal responses depended on remembered information about a distant goal. Motor processing can therefore represent actions at **multiple levels**, not merely sequences of joint angles. This supplies plausible *candidate units* for action indexing, but the experiment did not measure human concepts, perceived boundaries, or causal transfer from motor hierarchies to action thoughts.

\citet{schack:2006_representation} uses tennis serving to argue that experts organize **basic action concepts** for functionally important submovements into more coherent hierarchical groupings than novices. What is attractive here is *functional structure* rather than arbitrarily chosen cuts of a movement trajectory. The central caveat: the technique measures **perceptual–cognitive long-term memory association structures**, not necessarily online motor representations in the narrower sense. Shared expert knowledge of tennis also accounts for systematic clustering.

A useful complication comes from \citet{frank:2016_perceptualcognitive}. Novices physically practising golf putting improved performance, while combined **physical and mental practice** yielded clearer change in the measured action-related knowledge structure than physical practice alone. It would be premature to equate acquiring performance competence with acquiring a particular cognitive unit repertoire. Mental imagery itself may have motoric components, so this study does not license a clean motor/cognition dichotomy either.

## Does action expertise alter the perceived *units*?

Here are four experiments that most closely suggest that motor expertise may be relevant to segmentation, qualified by their actual outcomes.

- **Dance, \citet{blasing:2015_segmentation}:** professional dancers marked fewer boundaries in a novel contemporary dance phrase than physically active non-dancers. Eight amateur dancers later learned the phrase over around six weeks. Comparing their segmentation before and after learning found a reduction in **some middle trials** but **no significant overall change across all trials**. Very small intervention group, repeated observation and shared choreographic knowledge make a motor-specific conclusion unsafe.
- **Figure skating, \citet{levine:2017_goal}:** segmentation of skating routines aligned more with **ends of goal-directed subactions** than with beginnings. Prior skill expertise improved alignment relative to novices; short visual familiarization alone did not have an equivalent effect. This narrows the meaning of the boundaries, but does not match experts' richer knowledge of technique and goals against novices' conceptual learning.
- **Taekwondo, \citet{stadler:2021_shared}:** experts agreed more with each other about **where** meaningful boundaries should occur in filmed forms, including less familiar styles, even though experts and novices **did not significantly differ in average segmentation grain**. This seems closer to *which units*, rather than *how many*. Yet standardized technique vocabulary, goal knowledge and training conventions remain inseparable from motor expertise in the sample.
- **Ballet/Bharatanatyam, \citet{dinota:2020_expert}:** the paper's broad description of genre-specific expert segmentation must be tempered by the actual results: the significant early-trial expert reduction in boundary number was **found for Bharatanatyam**, **not comparably for ballet**. This selective effect and word-memory priming are consistent with genre-specific learned knowledge, but do not establish a general motoric contribution to action individuation.

These studies give the **most relevant correlations and suggestive training effects**. None independently measures a motor representation's unit structure and shows that it fixes which portion is judged to be *one action*.

Furthermore, \citet{pomiechowska:2017_motor} found that EEG mu suppression elicited during observation of hand actions depended on whether contextual cues favoured an **instrumental or communicative** interpretation. Motor recruitment might be partly *downstream* of interpreting the action. Mu suppression is an indirect and contentious motor marker, but the finding is enough to prohibit assuming that motor activation always creates, rather than reflects, the way an action is understood.

## What evidence would discriminate the hypothesis?

An experiment should dissociate **motor-outcome-based units** from (i) physical discontinuities, (ii) statistical sequence predictability, (iii) external goal and contact structure, and (iv) explicitly taught action concepts. Merely contrasting athletes with novices will not do.

A possible training design uses a novel smooth, repeatable manual sequence with six constituents. Groups encounter the **same observed motion, the same overall goal, the same object relations and the same instruction vocabulary**, but training is arranged to establish different **motor control groupings** (illustratively, **1–3 | 4–6** versus **1–2 | 3–4 | 5–6**). This is only plausible if training can create independently detectable motor subgoals or program boundaries *without altering the video stimuli used at test*; timed initiation costs, perturbation-dependent interference, transition effects or transfer might establish the motor organization. Simple verbal teaching of the partition is not a motor manipulation; and ordinary sequence chunking alone may not be *goal-directed motor action* organization.

After training, show both groups identical previously unseen instances of an actor performing the sequence. Collect a family of outcomes that distinguish *boundary detection* from *action individuation*: does a chosen interval count as **one complete action**? Can it be identified as **the same action** across different kinematic realizations and partial occlusion? Do descriptions and memory treat it as a coherent goal-directed unit, and is it represented as a subaction of a longer action? Include visual-only training, explicit conceptual-grouping instruction, and independently measured statistical expectations. Vary action outcomes where possible without simply changing motion cues.

The evidentially strong result would be that **verified differences in motor control structure** predict different partitions of the *same observed activity* into **action individuals**, *over and above* training-related conceptual, attentional, statistical and visual differences. An intervention that selectively reduces a motor representation's availability while preserving task comprehension would support a more demanding causal claim. The experience-mediated explanation of \citet{sinigaglia:2015_puzzle} would require further tests linking changes in action experience to these judgements; it should not be presupposed.

A publication-status audit is mandatory: an initially relevant, apparently rigorous study can be **retracted**, as the 2006 Hard–Lozano–Tversky study was. Such a study is not evidential support even where its original full text and BibTeX key have been verified.

## Assessment

**Promising but substantially unestablished.** There is good evidence that observers treat continuous human activity as hierarchically structured, that expertise sometimes changes preferred event boundaries, that motor control has multiple levels of goal-related representation, and that these structures can influence later action performance. The gap is a **causal bridge from *motor* organization to individuation *in thought***. It is still open whether a privileged action repertoire comes from learned goals, predictive event models, conceptual categories, sensorimotor control, or interactions among these.

The most important next move is to connect independently verified **motor subgoals/chunks** with measures of **action identity**, rather than accumulating further demonstrations of mirror activation or group differences in boundary counts. Empirically and theoretically, consider the converse direction—conceptual partonomies guiding motor control—as a genuine competitor.

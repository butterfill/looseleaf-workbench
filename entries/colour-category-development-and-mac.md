# Colour category development and mindreading as asynchronous coordination

## Motivating question

**Can the development of categorical colour perception and colour-word meanings illuminate the Mindreading as Asynchronous Coordination (MAC) account?** In particular, what is fixed by prelinguistic perceptual organization, what is calibrated by exposure to a linguistic community, and what would show that *coordination*, rather than the mere receipt of information, explains convergence on locally appropriate classifications?

A closely related question is whether colour provides an example of a classification practice with several perceptually admissible outcomes whose standards are learned socially and later applied alone.

## Working conclusion

Colour offers a credible developmental analogy for **constrained, socially calibrated, asynchronous classification**. Infants show coarse categorical responses before they learn colour words; this initial organization neither equals nor uniquely determines an adult lexicon. Children initially learn partial, often overextended word meanings, then gradually refine their boundaries in relation to the contrasting terms used in their language. Input frequency, adult naming consistency and targeted corrective feedback bear on this learning. Some perceptual-task responses also change alongside early lexical knowledge, although claims of causal sensory or categorical-perception reorganization outrun the evidence.

The key MAC limitation is **social calibration does not yet entail coordination**. Observational learning of a distribution of labels could explain much of the colour evidence. Corrective interaction gets closer to coordination, but its benefit is also compatible with better-directed attention. Thus colour illuminates the problem MAC addresses and suggests discriminating experiments; it does not independently establish MAC's specific mechanism \citep{ohanlon:2006_learning,apperly:2026_mindreading}.

## Keep three explananda apart

1. **Chromatic discrimination:** the visual ability to distinguish stimuli.
2. **Prelinguistic categorical responding:** infant recognition, habituation or search responses that differ depending on a proposed category boundary.
3. **Learning a language-specific lexical system:** acquiring a term's central uses, extension and contrasts with neighbouring terms.

These can develop on different timetables and be revealed by different tasks. An infant category effect does not entail a ready-made adult word meaning; a lexical effect on a task does not by itself show that a category appeared only with language. The developmental review \citet{maule:2023_development} is especially useful for preserving these distinctions.

## What precedes colour words?

The convergence of habituation, recognition-memory, search and related infant paradigms favours early categorical organization, although particular methods admit perceptual and attentional confounds. \citet{franklin:2014_case} explicitly separates evidence *for infant categories* from a stronger claim that they are hardwired, or that they determine the world's lexical colour systems.

In a large mapping study, \citet{skelton:2017_biological} analysed 179 four- to six-month-old infants. Responses across systematically sampled hues were consistent with approximately five categories at the tested lightness: red, yellow, green, blue and purple. Infant distinctions corresponded partly to cross-language lexical regularities and, relative to adaptation points, to axes of early retinogeniculate colour coding. This supports a **biological constraint**, not the conclusion that an eleven-term adult English partition is innate. The test sampled a constrained part of colour space and measured infant memory responses, not adult naming competence.

The older longitudinal comparison of English and Himba children \citep{roberson:2004_development} found that children who knew no colour terms made recognition errors largely according to perceptual distance; later, memory and focal-colour advantages increasingly tracked the relevant language. The authors treated this as evidence against predetermined adult-like lexical categories. Read alongside \citet{skelton:2017_biological}, its defensible implication is narrower: **a prelinguistic perceptual structure can exist without imposing mature lexical boundaries**. Absence of an eleven-category *memory* pattern is not absence of every form of infant category-sensitive responding.

There is also an important toddler result against a simple “words produce categories” sequence. \citet{Franklin:2005hp} found categorical responding in English toddlers regardless of colour-term competence, and in Himba toddlers who had not acquired their colour labels; more proficient naming did not produce a larger overall category effect. Together these studies favour a prelinguistic or early perceptual contribution while leaving room for subsequent lexical learning.

## What colour-word learners actually learn

The central developmental difficulty is not merely connecting *red* to a focal red sample. Children must learn what else *red* covers, when *orange*, *pink*, *purple* or *brown* is more appropriate, and how the network of terms partitions the space.

\citet{wagner:2013_slow} found that children's apparently mistaken labels frequently reflected systematic interim meanings: the same non-adult uses recurred across tasks, roughly three quarters of production errors were overextensions, and errors disproportionately involved nearby colours. A comprehension task supported broad lexical categories over an explanation exclusively in terms of pragmatic substitution during speaking. On their **slow-mapping** account, learning proceeds by gradually narrowing initial extensions as contrasting terms are acquired. Early systematic use is evidence of partial lexical knowledge, not random failure to attend to colour.

\citet{yang:2025_slow} reports a Japanese replication in 29 children aged about 2.5–3.8 years. Incorrect production pairs repeated consistently (47% against an estimated 17% chance); 78% of production errors were classified as overextensions, and proximal errors predominated in production (66% against 31% chance). Comprehension showed a smaller proximal-error excess (36% against 27% chance). The Japanese *ao* / *mizuiro* distinction illustrates why the learner cannot simply translate a fixed set of English colour categories. This replication lends support to slow mapping beyond English, but its small sample, fixed task order and relatively weak secondary comprehension effect limit its weight. The publication also contains an apparent count/percentage inconsistency noted in its [source assessment](../sources/colour-category-development-and-mac/yang2025_slow.md).

The emerging picture is **partial lexical anchors → broad interim meanings → contrast-sensitive boundary refinement**.

## What social input contributes

There are at least two distinguishable targets of learning:

- **Centroid mapping:** identifying a central or good exemplar for a term.
- **Boundary delineation:** determining the full extension and its borders with other terms.

In their German/Japanese comparison, \citet{saji:2025_crosslinguistic} found that frequency of a colour word in input robustly predicted German success with centroids; children's boundary delineation was worse for categories on which adults' naming was inconsistent. German boundary performance also depended on input frequency and category size. It would therefore overstate the evidence to say that frequency only teaches prototypes whereas adult consistency only teaches boundaries. A sentence in the article's discussion claims a German category-size effect on centroid mapping that is not supported by its reported Table 4 model; the model and abstract are used here. The Japanese data were previously collected and reanalysed for comparison, and samples were modest.

A more direct route from interaction to learning appears in \citet{ohanlon:2006_learning}. In experiments on novel secondary colour words, corrective feedback referred to the **child's own** mistaken label: after a choice, the tutor contrasted the target term with the label the child had supplied. Over five weeks, this response-contingent correction improved learning relative to relevant control and semantic-contrast conditions; the corrective–referential comparison was not uniformly significant. Replication with Italian children illustrated how the ambient lexicon changes the problem. Crucially, a third experiment found that making the colour dimension perceptually salient through task outcomes also improved learning to a similar extent. Thus the evidence identifies a role for information and attentional guidance in corrective interaction, but does not isolate coordination as the unique cause.

## Does lexical acquisition change colour perception?

The best answer is **perhaps in the organization and deployment of category-sensitive responses; the causal and sensory claims remain unsettled**.

\citet{franklin:2008_lateralization} found similar *magnitudes* of blue–green categorical responses in toddlers with and without well-mastered relevant labels, but a difference in the visual field in which the advantage appeared. This is compatible with a change in processing organization rather than the first appearance of categorization. However, split-field measures and adult lateralization effects have substantial reliability and interpretation problems \citep{maule:2023_development}.

With twelve- and nineteen-month-olds, \citet{forbes:2023_colour} found that parent-reported comprehension of *blue* or *green* was associated with some boundary-related gaze patterns and with changes in pupil dynamics. The key qualifications are unusually important:

- The authors say explicitly that Munsell-step matching does **not** establish perceptually equivalent steps, so **their experiments do not test categorical perception** in the strict sense.
- Knowledge groups were observational and correlated with age; age and knowledge were not entered together in the key first-experiment model.
- The looking effect differed by condition, and the second experiment found no corresponding colour-word effect in aggregate gaze duration.
- Colour preference, habituation and unmeasured background differences cannot be fully eliminated.

The careful conclusion is an **association between early word comprehension and responses to stimuli near the adult blue–green boundary**, not a demonstration that words causally restructure visual categories.

## How far the MAC analogy reaches

The MAC account argues that practical mindreading involves generating candidate mental-state ascriptions and selecting those other people would recognize as plausible and appropriate; standards of generation and selection are aligned through prior social experience, then used even when the mindreader is alone \citep{apperly:2026_mindreading}. This is its asynchronous component.

Colour provides a possible developmental counterpart:

| Colour | Proposed MAC counterpart |
|---|---|
| Sensory structure restricts plausible divisions but does not uniquely specify mature lexical categories. | Behaviour, rational constraints and mental-state concepts restrict plausible ascriptions without uniquely fixing the appropriate one. |
| Children acquire partial word meanings before adult-like extensions. | Learners may possess candidate interpretations before mastering when they are socially apt. |
| Different languages partition colours differently within perceptual constraints. | Groups may converge on different, locally appropriate interpretive standards. |
| Frequency, contrast structure and adult naming consistency shape lexical acquisition. | Patterns and consistency of others' interpretations may tune which mental states are generated and selected. |
| Correction tied to a child's own classification can promote learning. | Disagreement, repair and feedback may align interpretations across interactions. |
| Learned colour terms are later applied without an interlocutor. | Earlier social alignment guides later solitary mindreading. |

These are **structural and developmental analogies**, not evidence that the same mechanisms implement colour naming and mindreading. Colour norms can be stated explicitly and externally checked, while mindreading plausibility standards may remain implicit and context-sensitive.

A further distinction matters for the separate question of **norm-like maintenance**. Evidence that community usage predicts the learner's categories does not establish that the categories exist because individuals actively uphold the practice. The experiments document acquisition and calibration; they do not establish the function or motivation of socially maintaining a norm.

## Discriminating experiment

The most direct test would compare **interactive adjustment** with **yoked informational exposure** in novel colour-category learning.

One learner would coordinate classifications with a tutor whose correction, clarification and repair depend on that learner's choices. A yoked learner would see the same stimuli, labels, corrections and outcomes generated by the interaction, but these would not respond to *their* choices. Later tests, performed alone, would measure generalization to untrained colours, learned boundaries, retention, uncertainty at borderline cases and predictions of how that tutor's group would classify them.

A specifically interaction-based account predicts an advantage of participating in the alignment over informationally matched observation. A stronger MAC analogue would train with **two communities** using different but perceptually admissible boundaries and test whether the learner retrieves the locally relevant standard according to the anticipated interlocutor. The alternative to test is that frequency, distributional learning and attention suffice without genuinely reciprocal coordination.

## Outstanding questions

1. **Mechanism:** Does interaction supply diagnostic evidence, or does mutual adjustment contribute something over and above matched input?
2. **Representation:** Which apparent language effects reflect sensory discrimination, category-sensitive attention, recognition memory, lexical decisions or task strategy?
3. **Generalization:** Do socially acquired boundaries extend to novel hues and transfer to tasks without naming?
4. **Community dependence:** Can participants acquire and switch between distinct local standards without merely memorizing interlocutor labels?
5. **Measurement:** How stable and replicable are reported lateralization and infant pupil/gaze effects?
6. **Literature coverage:** The primary papers surveyed here were checked, but the broader literature and several relevant works cited in the 2023 review have not yet been independently assessed. This entry remains a **workbench investigation, not a promoted synthesis**.

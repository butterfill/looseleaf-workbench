## The proposal in one paragraph

**Speech is promising principally as a tractable test case of the intention–motor interface, rather than as an occasion to resurrect the classical motor theory of perception.** Spoken action connects communicative intentions, lexical choices, phonological organisation, phonetic/articulatory planning, predicted acoustic and somatosensory outcomes, and continuously controlled bodily movement. These levels can be independently studied to a degree unusual in the wider action literature. The strongest immediate opportunity is to distinguish (i) coordination or *matching* of outcomes, (ii) the *selection* and parameterisation of a specific realisation, and (iii) acquisition of the capacity to connect the two. The more ambitious possibility is that speech could test whether motor representation gives experience an event-based structure and thereby makes new action concepts available for thought. This last possibility is philosophically significant but weakly established empirically.

## Connection to the existing interface problem

In \citet{butterfill:2012_intention}, some actions are guided by both propositional intentions and motor representations specifying outcomes. Their specifications **match** when, in context, occurrence of one set of outcomes would normally constitute or (partially) cause the other; exact identity is unnecessary. The problem is why non-accidental matching occurs despite differences in representational format. An initially proposed route involves action concepts whose reference is fixed by **deference to motor representation**, rather than a shared inferential code; the stronger conjecture that *all* matching requires such deference was explicitly presented as speculative.

The revised [Motor in Thought book proposal](https://github.com/butterfill/motor-in-thought/blob/master/proposal/Book%20proposal_revised.tex) extends the question to three aspects: performing one's own action, representing observed action, and acting together. Its positive proposal is that motor representation helps give experience an event-based organisation that can shape the concepts and thoughts available for action guidance. The [chapter outlines](https://github.com/butterfill/motor-in-thought/blob/master/proposal/Chapter_outlines_revised.tex) distinguish the representational-format problem, existing solutions, the structure of experience, and a prospective solution.

A more specific antecedent was found in [the earlier chapter-outline text](https://github.com/butterfill/motor-in-thought/blob/master/chapter-outlines-for-proposal/content.tex): **phonetic actions already serve as the illustration** of a proposed event-based contribution of motor representation to perception. Their acoustic and visible effects vary with context, rate and dialect; the conjecture is that the effects are nevertheless experienced as belonging to a phonetic action or event. The same draft explicitly notes a gap between early phonetic discrimination and the much later ability to think explicitly about phonetic gestures. Recovering and testing this existing proposal is more promising than adding speech as a merely decorative example.

The [interface-problem source knowledge base](https://github.com/butterfill/kb-interface-problem-sources) distinguishes matching/selection, content/format/vehicle, executable/observational concepts, reusable motor schemas/token motor plans, development/performance, and motor-format hybridity. Its claim records provide orientation, not an independent re-reading of all cited experiments. Particularly relevant accounts include \citep{mylopoulos:2016_intentions,burnston:2017_interface,christensen:2021_skill,fridland:2021_intention,brossel:2026_solving,mylopoulos:2025_motoric}.

## Findings from the speech literature discussed in the conversation

### 1. Gestures persist in speech production; the classical motor-perception thesis remains unsettled

Three claims must be kept apart: (a) speech production is organised around articulatory gestures; (b) perception interacts with speech motor mechanisms; (c) what is perceived is the speaker's intended gesture. The first remains a serious theoretical programme (Articulatory Phonology, \citep{Browman:1992da,Goldstein:2003bn}); the second has causal support; the third does **not** follow from either. The distinction between intended motor commands, distal articulatory events and perceived acoustic/phonological properties is substantive. The tripartite analysis in \citep{galantucci:2006_motor} is a useful point of departure.

In Articulatory Phonology, gestures are dynamically specified vocal-tract tasks (e.g. forming a labial closure), not fixed sets of muscle contractions; they can overlap temporally. Goldstein et al. (2007) report articulatory measurements of speech errors bearing on the hypothesis that gestures are compositional production units. This is not a demonstration that intended gestures are the objects of auditory perception.

Motor contributions to listening are evidenced by articulator-specific stimulation and interference effects \citep{smalle:2015_dissociating}, and by motor constraints on infant speech perception \citep{bruderer:2015_sensorimotor}; Skipper et al. (2017) offer a wider review. But speech perception can survive substantial speech-production impairment: a patient with impaired articulation retained speech-sound discrimination while struggling with explicit labelling \citep{stasenko:2015_causal}. Direct recordings show acoustic–phonetic sensitivity in superior temporal cortex \citep{mesgarani:2014_phonetic}. A 2026 experiment by Carneiro Pereira et al. links speech-like silent lip movements, rather than mere overlapping muscle use, with selective changes in sound discrimination, but does not show obligatory motor simulation for listening.

### 2. Categorical identification should not be mistaken for an exclusively categorical perceptual code

McMurray (2022) argues that classical categorical-perception findings have been overinterpreted: forced-choice identification boundaries and some task-dependent discrimination effects do not establish that listeners lose fine-grained within-category information. Toscano et al. (2010) provide evidence of graded auditory encoding alongside categorisation. Phonetic categories remain real features of processing; the stronger claim that perception is *only* categorical is under pressure.

For the older argument about 'hearing gestures', this weakens the inference from correspondence between categories and articulations to gestures as perceptual objects. Similarity could arise through learned acoustics, categorical decisions or coupled sensorimotor prediction. The user's 2010 *Categorical Perception: A Fresh Start* draft had already questioned the standard operational definition and entertained coexistence of categorical and non-categorical processing; this deserves closer comparison with McMurray rather than a simple story of refutation.

### 3. Speech production makes intermediate interfaces accessible

Levelt (2001) distinguishes conceptual preparation, lexical selection, phonological encoding, phonetic encoding and articulation. This is an explanatory architecture, not a reason to identify phonological representations with propositional intentions or to suppose that production is strictly serial. It suggests **multiple interface questions**, not necessarily one leap from intention to muscle.

Computational speech models provide unusually explicit motor-side machinery: DIVA/GODIVA describe speech-sound motor programmes and sequencing (Meier & Guenther, 2023), while FACTS gives hierarchical control of vocal-tract tasks, articulator state estimates, sensory prediction and feedback (Parrell et al., 2019). Crucially, modelling how an already specified utterance or sound is executed is not yet explaining how a communicative intention recruits precisely that motor structure. Representational transitions should be traced one by one, with careful attention to which transitions are actually computationally implemented.

The complexity of overlapping gestures also makes speech a useful case for the proposal that motor format combines discrete/symbolic organisation with continuous/analog dynamics \citep{mylopoulos:2025_motoric}. The presence of different *levels of specificity* alone is insufficient to establish different *formats*.

### 4. Feedback perturbations reveal constraints flowing between linguistic and motor levels

Niziolek & Guenther (2013) report substantially stronger compensation for perturbed auditory feedback near vowel-category boundaries than farther from them. Bourguignon et al. (2014) found that adaptation can depend on whether the altered auditory feedback crosses a word/nonword boundary. These are instances of linguistic or perceptual structure constraining speech sensorimotor adjustment; **neither identifies a propositional intention directly controlling motor representation**.

A striking 2026 experiment (Parrell et al.) reports adaptation after *cancelled*, planned speech accompanied by suitably perturbed playback, while passive listening lacked the same effect. This supports a planning-related sensory-prediction route to motor learning that does not require the planned utterance to be executed. It narrows the motor-side explanation, but the relationship between the task instruction, the plan and any propositional intention remains open. The original study should be checked in detail before using it to arbitrate philosophical theories.

These experiments afford concrete tests of what is specified as a **target**: a constriction, a phonetic category, an auditory outcome, a lexical object, or some context-dependent combination. They also motivate separating (1) correspondence between higher and lower specifications, (2) selection of a particular feasible implementation, and (3) correction of an unfolding implementation.

### 5. Production–perception plasticity and inner speech bear on experience, but do not establish its proposed structure

Lametti et al. (2014) report that adaptation in producing speech can shift subsequent phonetic perception. Such findings support an influence of motor learning on perceptual processing; they do not establish that perception represents phonetic events *as articulatory actions*, or that these event representations make action concepts possible. Grandchamp et al. (2019) distinguish expanded from condensed forms of inner speech, raising doubts about any simple identification of thinking in words with fully specified covert articulation.

The book's stronger, experience-mediated proposal needs **developmental and task-transfer evidence**: does acquiring a new speech-production skill give an agent a newly accessible way of *thinking about and intentionally controlling* a type of action, beyond improving recognition or pronunciation? Does the effect require motorically structured experience, rather than learned associations between independently available phonetic concepts and motor schemas?

### 6. Conversational interaction extends the scope, but is probably a second-stage question

Speech also joins producing one's own utterance with perceiving another's and coordinating conversation. Bögels et al. (2015) report evidence for answer planning before an incoming question has finished. The relevance to joint action is genuine, but early response planning does not demonstrate a motor representation of *collective goals*. A conversational extension should follow a clearer production-side account, not substitute for one.

## Candidate philosophical payoffs and rival explanations

| Candidate explanation | What a speech case might discriminate | Outstanding difficulty |
|---|---|---|
| Reference by deference \citep{butterfill:2012_intention} | Whether learning to produce a novel contrast makes available an action concept whose reference depends on a motor representation | New intentional control could arise through learned associations instead |
| Executable concepts / motor schemas \citep{mylopoulos:2016_intentions} | Whether phonological planning recruits a reusable schema that is then contextually parameterised | Demonstrating schema activation does not establish the format or structure of the action concept |
| Dimensional biasing \citep{burnston:2017_interface} | Whether linguistic goals narrow a range of viable phonetic or auditory outcomes without selecting one concrete motor plan | Lexical bias need not be the causal contribution of an intention |
| Conceptual-space matching and Bayesian selection \citep{brossel:2026_solving} | Whether general target regions, possible articulatory trajectories and selection can be separately modelled | The proposed metric and selection computations remain theoretical commitments |
| Learned coordination / translation \citep{christensen:2021_skill} | Whether training produces reliable cross-level mappings even in the absence of conscious action imagery | A working mapping does not alone explain the origin of motor-shaped concepts |
| Experience-mediated shaping of thought (the *Motor in Thought* proposal) | Whether motor learning changes experience of phonetic *events* and thereby makes new intentional distinctions available | Motor effects on discrimination, imagery or skill do not by themselves show this mediation |

This comparison is a **research agenda**, not a conclusion that the extant experiments have already isolated these mechanisms.

## Promising research questions

1. **Production-side interface (highest immediate return).** Exactly which representations carry constraints from phonological planning to auditory targets and articulatory control? Where do models implement these constraints, and where do they simply assume an upstream plan? Use feedback perturbations and gestural timing to distinguish matching from selection, and from error correction.
2. **Learning to intend newly mastered speech actions (highest philosophical payoff).** Compare perceptual-only and articulatory training on a novel phonetic contrast. Test not merely pronunciation and classification but flexible, context-sensitive *intentional selection* of an articulatory contrast and transfer to unfamiliar contexts. Ask whether changed perceptual event organisation mediates this gain.
3. **Phonetic gestures as objects of perception (highest conceptual risk).** Test whether listeners track a gesture as a persisting event across changing acoustic and visual manifestations, and whether motor constraints matter beyond auditory statistics. Avoid inferring perceived content merely from motor-cortex recruitment.
4. **Extension to dialogue and joint speech.** Later investigate the simultaneous monitoring of another's speech and preparation of one's own, while distinguishing complementary individual goals from genuinely collective outcomes.

## Uncertainties and potential defeaters

- **Speech may be unrepresentative:** a linguistically learned hierarchy of codes might create an unusually easy connection between thought and movement that does not generalise to grasping or other action.
- **Many speech-production goals are not intentions:** a syllable, category, lexical entry or auditory target should not silently be promoted to a propositional intention. A communicative intention may leave wording open; an intention to pronounce a specific expression is a different case.
- **Perceptual effects underdetermine perceptual content:** motor modulation of hearing does not show that we hear articulatory gestures, much less intended motor commands. The *direct realist* distal-event claim and the classical *motor* intention claim must remain distinct.
- **Category decisions are not categorical perception in the strong sense:** the contemporary critique concerns perceptual encoding, not the existence of categories or categorical responses.
- **Evidence for interaction is not evidence for a format bridge:** temporal sequence, neural coactivation, feedback compensation and computational connection can all occur without establishing translation, deference, shared format or conceptual dependence.
- **Acquisition and online performance must be separated:** a motorically grounded route to learning an action concept could coexist with later use without motor imagery. Conversely, an online sensorimotor effect says little about how a concept was learned.
- **Source verification remains incomplete:** several newer publications were read only through abstracts, summaries or selected passages during this conversation; key experiments and their alternative interpretations require full-text checking. See [the source checklist](../sources/speech-actions-interface-problem/checklist.md).
- **No general consensus solution:** the interface-problem accounts above propose distinct architectures; their strongest claims should not be treated as experimentally established simply because speech exemplifies coordination.

## Provisional direction

An independently worthwhile paper could ask **“Saying What You Intend: Speech Production and the Interface Problem.”** Its initial claim would be methodological and substantive: speech reveals a *hierarchy* of cross-format and cross-level coordination problems, with distinct demands of matching, selection, feedback and acquisition. A second step would test the more ambitious thesis that motor representation structures experiences of phonetic actions in a way that enables new forms of thought and intentional control.

A focussed investigation of the **phonological-planning → articulatory-target interface** looks more promising than starting with a defence of the motor theory of speech perception. It is worth pursuing as a test case, not yet as the foundation of a wholesale account of intention.

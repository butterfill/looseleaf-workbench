# Are phonetic gestures the basic units of speech perception?

## The question

The revised motor theory of speech perception proposed that the listener perceives the speaker's *intended phonic gestures*, rather than merely the acoustic signal. Does contemporary research support this proposal? In particular, are gestures the common units of speech *production and perception*, or does motor activity contribute to a perceptual process whose objects and representations may be principally auditory?

The most defensible current answer distinguishes three propositions:

1. **Gestural organisation of production:** speech can be analysed as overlapping, dynamically specified articulatory gestures. This remains an important research programme.
2. **Sensorimotor contributions to listening:** production-related systems sometimes make measurable and causally manipulable contributions to discrimination or categorisation of speech sounds. This is well supported, though the circumstances and functions differ across studies.
3. **Gestures as objects of perception:** the listener fundamentally perceives actual articulatory events, or the speaker's intended motor structures, rather than acoustic patterns or phonological categories. This is a distinct and much less settled claim.

The stronger motor theory does not follow from the first two propositions. Neither an articulatory organisation of production nor recruitment of motor circuitry fixes *what a perception is of*. Conversely, rejecting obligatory motor reconstruction does not refute every account on which articulatory events are perceived.

## What counts as a gesture?

In \citet{Liberman:1985bn}, a gesture is not merely the motion of a lip or tongue; the relevant intended gesture is a more remote motor structure controlling such movement. The revised theory posits a specialised phonetic system capable of recovering intended gestural structure from an acoustic pattern whose components are overlapped and highly context-sensitive. Thus it combines a thesis about perceptual *objects*, a thesis about mechanisms, and a thesis about speech-specific processing.

**Articulatory Phonology** develops a related but distinguishable conception. In \citet{Browman:1992da}, gestures are dynamical vocal-tract tasks, such as making a lip closure, with intrinsic temporal organisation. A gesture is not individuated by a single set of muscle movements: different articulator configurations may achieve the relevant constriction. Utterances are *constellations* of partly overlapping gestures. The theory aims to explain coarticulation, connected-speech alternations, phonological contrast, speech errors and development within a unified action-based account. More recent work extends the dynamical framework to prosodic organisation, timing and prominence \citep{byrd:2021_cracking}.

The distinction between *motor intentions or commands* and *public articulatory actions* matters. \citet{Goldstein:2003bn} explicitly treat speech forms as public actions that are produced and perceived, a perspective closer to direct realism than to a model requiring recovery of private motor commands. An intended action and the articulatory event performed can diverge. There is no single unqualified 'gestural theory' to which all findings can be assigned.

\citet{Galantucci:2006mo} usefully separate three historical claims: that speech processing is special, that perceiving speech is perceiving gestures, and that speech perception recruits the motor system. They judge the first difficult to sustain, while treating the latter two as serious hypotheses. That separation remains essential.

## Why the older inference from categorical perception is in trouble

Section III of \citet{Butterfill:2009vs}, *Seeing Causes and Hearing Gestures*, reconstructs an explanatory argument roughly as follows:

1. Speech perception exhibits category boundaries and context-sensitive constancy.
2. The boundaries correspond to contrasts between intended phonic gestures.
3. Both the boundaries and their correspondence to gestures need explanation.
4. A system that recovers the speaker's intended gestures provides the best explanation; therefore the objects of speech perception are gestures.

The original attractions are clear. An acoustic cue can change greatly without a change of phonetic identity, and coarticulated gestures do not leave simple independent pieces of signal to decode. A phonetic category that stays relatively stable over changes of talker or rate invites explanation.

But even a correspondence between categories and gesture types does not uniquely establish gestural *content*. The acoustic effects of articulations are structured, and listeners can learn probabilistic relations among many acoustic cues and speaker-dependent distributions. The 'ideal adapter' model of \citet{kleinschmidt:2015_robust} is an explicit alternative explanation of robust, context-sensitive interpretation in terms of learned generative models and rapid adaptation. The existence of this sort of explanation changes the competition among explanations, even without settling it.

More importantly, the classical *categorical perception* result cannot bear the theoretical weight placed on it. \citet{mcmurray:2022_myth} argues that the standard relation between steep identification curves and peaks in discrimination is partly a product of task demands, and that extensive evidence against loss of within-category detail undermines even some weaker versions of classical categorical perception. His claim is stronger than the uncontroversial observation that categories affect phonetic decisions: it contests the inference to a categorical *perceptual encoding*.

In an ERP experiment, \citet{toscano:2010_continuous} found early N1 responses varying approximately linearly with voice-onset time, while later P3 responses were affected by phonological category. Fine-grained acoustic differences were therefore encoded despite discrete phoneme judgments. A result about the *categorisation of* a signal should not be treated without further argument as a result about its exclusively categorical representation.

The unpublished manuscript *Categorical Perception: A Fresh Start* (supplied with this inquiry) was already concerned that ordinary operational definitions do not supply a neutral theoretical characterization. Its suggestion that category-sensitive and more continuous processing might coexist remains a useful starting point, but should be reassessed against \citet{mcmurray:2022_myth}'s detailed methodological objections. A modest category effect must not quietly be renamed categorical perception in the strong classical sense.

**Result for the older gestural argument:** there may be real categorical *effects* and real gesture–category correlations. Yet neither makes it compulsory to posit an exclusively gestural perceptual code, still less a specialised module identifying the speaker's intended commands.

## Evidence that the motor system matters

The crucial distinction is between motor *activation* while listening and evidence that intervening on the motor system changes performance.

- \citet{dausilio:2009_motor} applied TMS to lip- and tongue-related motor representations during discrimination of speech sounds articulated by the corresponding effectors. They report an articulator-specific double dissociation in facilitation and interference. This is evidence for a functionally specific motor contribution, not simply simultaneous motor activity.
- \citet{smalle:2015_dissociating} separately analysed sensitivity and response bias. Disrupting motor-lip cortex reduced discrimination sensitivity, while disrupting the motor-hand representation affected response bias instead. This is important precisely because task-dependent motor involvement need not all be *perceptual*.
- \citet{bruderer:2015_sensorimotor} temporarily restricted the tongue position of six-month-old infants using teething toys, checked articulatory restriction by ultrasound, and found impaired discrimination of a non-native contrast when the relevant tongue movements were constrained. Motor-related influence is possible before fluent production and even for a contrast not learned through ordinary native-language production.

These experiments count against the assertion that production-related bodily and neural resources are wholly irrelevant to listening. They do not establish the stronger claim that a normal listener invariably identifies a phonetic contrast by recreating the corresponding motor commands. Differences between natural and noisy stimuli, response selection, somatosensory consequences, attention and task demands remain possible moderators.

The case study by \citet{stasenko:2015_causal} points in the other direction. An individual with severely impaired speech production following a left frontal lesion showed intact speech-sound discrimination and largely preserved everyday auditory language comprehension, despite marked difficulty labelling the discriminated sounds by phonemic category. The case distinguishes *hearing a difference* from *explicitly identifying and naming the category*. A single dissociation does not settle the entire architecture, but it conflicts with the idea that intact production circuitry is always necessary for perceptual discrimination.

## The auditory system can represent phonetic structure

\citet{mesgarani:2014_phonetic} recorded cortical responses during listening and found phonetic-feature organisation in human superior temporal gyrus, grounded in the acoustic spectrotemporal characteristics of speech. This supplies a positive account of significant phonetic processing outside motor cortex; it does not by itself say whether gestures might also be represented elsewhere.

In lesion-symptom mapping of 73 left-hemisphere stroke patients, \citet{binder:2025_lesion} identified regions including Heschl's gyrus, nearby temporal-insular and auditory belt regions, and posterior superior temporal sulcus as critical for normal acoustic-phonetic discrimination, with variation across place, manner, voicing and vowel contrasts. The study is important evidence that auditory temporal mechanisms are not passive recipients waiting for a motor analysis to determine phonetic structure.

A broader neurobiological proposal is therefore one of **interacting auditory and production systems**, with context-dependent and potentially predictive motor contributions, rather than an exclusively auditory or obligatorily motor code. The systematic review and meta-analysis by \citet{skipper:2017_hearing} defends a distributed, dynamically organised network picture, though its interpretation is not universal consensus.

## What recent work has added

Two newer findings are particularly informative, though of different evidential kinds.

Using MEG during phonological discrimination, \citet{perron:2024_left} found left ventral primary motor and bilateral posterior superior temporal activity sensitive to phonological differences. Contrary to one straightforward compensation hypothesis, increasing noise did **not** correspondingly increase the reported frontal motor contribution. This is evidence for auditory–motor interaction, but MEG source activity is not itself a causal manipulation and cannot demonstrate motor necessity or gestural perceptual content.

\citet{carneiropereira:2026_origin} tested interference from **specific silent lip movements** while listeners discriminated syllable pairs in noise. Opening and closing the lips selectively impeded discrimination involving a labial contrast; protruding and retracting the lips, although recruiting partly overlapping musculature, did not produce the same selective effect. Finger movement supplied a comparison baseline. This favours an articulator- or gesture-relevant account over mere general depletion of motor resources, and complicates explanations appealing solely to phonological or auditory imagery. But mouth movement generates motor and somatosensory signals; the experiment did not show that perceived objects are the speaker's intended gestures.

The review by \citet{tseng:2026_embodied} develops the sensorimotor case in native and non-native phoneme perception and learning, including degraded or unfamiliar input. Its discussion of *manual* gestures used in phoneme training should be kept separate from the phonetic *articulatory* gestures that are the candidate units of speech.

Thus the growing evidence for sensorimotor interaction is welcome news for **some** versions of gestural theories but does not vindicate the classical motor theory in its entirety.

## Where the philosophical question remains

The evidence leaves several possibilities open:

- **Motor contribution without gestural content:** articulatory models help anticipate, disambiguate or classify acoustic events; what is perceptually represented need not itself be a gesture.
- **Mixed representation:** perceptual processing preserves graded acoustics and category information while sometimes representing articulatory structure.
- **Gesture perception:** the listener perceives the articulatory event responsible for the signal. This is compatible with the motor system contributing contingently, or even not being necessary in some tasks.
- **Intended-command perception:** what is perceived is the speaker's intended motor organisation rather than the action actually executed. This stronger claim needs its own evidence.

These claims make different predictions, and their truth should not be inferred directly from localisation of neural activation. Perceptual *content*, representational *format*, *causal mechanism* and the units of speech *production* are different explananda.

A decisive research design would try to dissociate intended articulation, realised articulation, acoustic evidence and bodily feedback, while holding phonetic categories and response requirements constant where possible. It would compare ordinary speech with noise-degraded speech; measure both discrimination sensitivity and response bias; examine implicit acoustic sensitivity as well as explicit labelling; and selectively perturb gesture-specific motor representations. A gesture-selective perceptual effect that survives matched acoustics, learned cue expectations and response-bias controls would strengthen a gestural-representation hypothesis. Inferring that the *object* of experience is a gesture would still require an additional theory of what makes representations perceptual.

## Assessment and reading order

**Production gestures remain important; motor contributions to perception are genuine; the claim that intended gestures are the basic perceptual objects is unresolved.** The deepest change since the classical debate may be that the old categorical-perception premise has become much less secure even as better intervention evidence for motor involvement has emerged.

An efficient route back into the literature is:

1. \citet{mcmurray:2022_myth} and \citet{toscano:2010_continuous} — reconsider the categorical-perception premise.
2. \citet{Liberman:1985bn} and \citet{Galantucci:2006mo} — recover and disaggregate the classical theoretical commitments.
3. \citet{Browman:1992da}, \citet{Goldstein:2003bn}, and \citet{byrd:2021_cracking} — articulate the gestural production and public-action alternatives.
4. \citet{smalle:2015_dissociating} and \citet{stasenko:2015_causal} — examine intervention and dissociation.
5. \citet{skipper:2017_hearing}, \citet{binder:2025_lesion}, \citet{carneiropereira:2026_origin}, and \citet{tseng:2026_embodied} — assess interactive-network evidence and the recent research frontier.

This is a **focused workbench capture** of the research question, not a systematic review. In particular, no strong inference is drawn from raw motor activation, from a single patient, from a single ERP task, or from a phoneme identification boundary alone.

# Slow mapping: Color word learning as a gradual inductive process

**Citation:** \citet{wagner:2013_slow} — Katie Wagner, Karen Dobkins and David Barner (2013), *Cognition*. DOI: 10.1016/j.cognition.2013.01.010.

**Question.** When children produce colour words but do not yet use them with adult-like extensions, is the obstacle failing to abstract colour as a dimension, or learning where words' boundaries lie?

**Evidence.** Experiment 1 elicited labels for colour samples in two tasks and classified non-adult responses by **consistency, overextension and perceptual proximity**. Most errors were systematic. The General Discussion reports that about 75% of production errors were overextensions: a term applied to its correct focal sample was also applied to neighbouring samples. Proximity errors occurred disproportionately often even among children with very limited adult-like colour vocabulary. Experiment 2 used comprehension choices to test whether overextension was just a speaking strategy; proximal errors above chance supported genuinely broad meanings.

**Interpretation.** Children apparently have abstracted colour sufficiently to form partial hypotheses when they begin using colour words. Their language-specific extensions can then be gradually refined as exemplars and contrasting labels accumulate: **slow mapping**.

**Limits.** A staged inductive process is inferred mainly from samples grouped by current knowledge and from patterned errors; the experiments do not directly trace continuous boundary movement within the same individuals over years. Task-specific coding of chance and perceptual proximity matters. The model is much stronger than the claim that every child follows an identical path.

**Use here.** Crucial source for partial categories between prelinguistic vision and stable community-specific lexical partitions.

# Biological origins of color categorization

**Citation:** \citet{skelton:2017_biological} — Alice E. Skelton and colleagues (2017), *Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences*, 114, 5545–5550. DOI: 10.1073/pnas.1612881114.

**Design.** A large infant recognition-memory study systematically samples hues corresponding to a row of the World Color Survey colour array. The final analysed sample was 179 infants aged approximately four to six months. Analyses infer category divisions from patterns of responses as hue changes.

**Findings.** At the tested lightness, responses are consistent with about five categorical regions, conventionally described as red, yellow, green, blue and purple. The locations of infant divisions show partial correspondence to recurring divisions and centroids in cross-linguistic colour naming. Their positions relative to adaptation points are consistent with cardinal axes of early retinogeniculate coding. The authors argue that the recurrence of lexical divisions is partly constrained by perceptual biology.

**Limits.** This is a pattern of **infant categorical recognition-memory responses**, not a demonstration that infants possess an adult lexical system. The sampling is restricted in lightness and does not cover the entirety of colour space. Agreement with a model of early colour coding is suggestive of biological constraint, not proof of a unique genetic or causal origin of lexical categories.

**Use here.** Strongest direct evidence against treating perceptual colour structure as wholly arbitrarily imposed by language; consistent with multiple socially learned mature partitions.

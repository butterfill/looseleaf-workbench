# The case for infant colour categories

**Citation:** \citet{franklin:2014_case} — Anna Franklin, Alice Skelton and Gemma Catchpole (2014), chapter in *Colour Studies: A Broad Spectrum*, pp. 169–180.

**Purpose and argument.** The authors review evidence that infants group hues categorically before colour-term learning, across habituation, search and related paradigms. Their central argument is that results from multiple paradigms are hard to dismiss collectively as artefacts of perceptual differences or other non-categorical explanations. The chapter is a defence of **early categories**, while deliberately separating that claim from their origin and relationship to adult colour terms.

**Useful detail.** In §6, the authors say that infant categories may influence the evolution of colour lexicons or scaffolding of word learning, but they may equally be independent of lexical categories. They expressly reject the inference from infant category effects to a fixed, universally hardwired set of mature colour categories.

**What it does not establish.** This is a chapter reviewing several earlier experiments, not a new large-scale mapping study. The reviewed findings cannot uniquely identify a biological mechanism or show that infant boundaries constrain every language.

**Use in this inquiry.** Supports the first half of **constrained but underdetermining perceptual structure**, and establishes why later social calibration is a separate explanatory question.

# The development of color categories in two languages: A longitudinal study

**Citation:** \citet{roberson:2004_development} — Debi Roberson, Jules Davidoff, Ian R. L. Davies and Laura R. Shapiro (2004), *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General*, 133, 554–571.

**Design.** English and Himba children were followed longitudinally for roughly three years. Production, comprehension and colour-recognition memory were assessed in populations with substantially different lexicons, environments and educational contexts.

**Results.** Both groups acquired terms slowly and with marked individual variation. Among children meeting the study's criteria for no colour-term knowledge, memory confusions predominantly tracked physical perceptual distance, not the eleven named English regions; no specifically adult English-like focal memory advantage was found. After colour terms began to be acquired, focal advantages and memory confusions were increasingly aligned with the child's language.

**Argument and limits.** The authors interpret the result as development from a perceptually driven continuum towards culturally appropriate categories, challenging a predetermined English-like eleven-category scheme. Their **memory-error test is not equivalent** to the habituation or recognition methods that later yielded infant category effects. The “no knowledge” group also cannot be assumed to have had no relevant linguistic exposure at all. The results should therefore be used against fixed *mature* categories rather than as proof of a wholly uncategorized infant colour space.

**Use here.** An important longitudinal complement to prelinguistic category research: shared early perceptual constraints leave substantive room for language-specific learning.

# \citet{kuhl:1975_speech} — chinchilla voicing boundaries without human gestures

**Source:** Kuhl and Miller (1975), *Speech Perception by the Chinchilla: Voiced-Voiceless Distinction in Alveolar Plosive Consonants*, *Science* 190. DOI: [10.1126/science.1166301](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1126/science.1166301). Research ref: papers:kuhl:1975_speech.

## Experiments and interpretation

Four chinchillas learned to distinguish natural voiced versus voiceless syllables (/d/ and /t/) across several speakers and vowels. They generalised to new tokens including synthetic consonants, then were tested along synthetic voice-onset-time (VOT) continua; other animal listeners and English-speaking adults provided comparison (*Experiments 1–2*, pp. 69–71). The animals' identification functions had human-like VOT boundaries.

## Philosophical significance

The original motor-theory inference from sharply changing identification judgments to specifically human perception of **intended phonetic motor commands** is weaker if closely similar boundaries arise in a non-speaking mammal. Auditory sensitivities, learning and species-general categorisation offer alternative explanations.

But 'labels' for a chinchilla are operationally conditioned responses, **not evidence of phonemic concepts**, perceptual phenomenology or exactly human speech categories. Nor does the study show impossibility of perceiving the external event producing the sound: direct realists can propose auditory pickup of actions by nonhuman animals. The claim is narrower: a boundary alone cannot uniquely evidence human motor reconstruction.

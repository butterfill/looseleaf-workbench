# \citet{krieger-redwood:2013_selective} — phoneme judgments versus comprehension under TMS

**Source:** Krieger-Redwood, Gaskell, Lindsay and Jefferies (2013), *The Selective Role of Premotor Cortex in Speech Perception: A Contribution to Phoneme Judgements but not Speech Comprehension*, *Journal of Cognitive Neuroscience* 25. DOI: [10.1162/jocn_a_00463](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1162/jocn_a_00463). Research ref: papers:krieger-redwood:2013_selective.

## Causal comparison

The study applies offline repetitive TMS to three sites—premotor cortex (PMC), posterior superior temporal gyrus (pSTG), and occipital pole—then tests auditory-word processing. Crucially, the **same heard words** are used for explicit final-phoneme choices (e.g. /t/ vs /p/) and semantic category choices (e.g. natural vs man-made); a visual matching control is included (*Methods—Tasks, Stimulation Parameters*). Fifteen participants furnished the reported task comparison.

PMC stimulation significantly slowed explicit phonological decisions but **not semantic decisions**. pSTG stimulation disrupted both language decisions, whereas the occipital-pole control disrupted neither. The Task × TMS interaction at PMC supports a selective task effect (*Results*, p. 2184; *Discussion*, pp. 2185–87).

## Contribution to the core inference

This is a causal distinction between phoneme-level *judgment/awareness* and access to word meaning, not merely an activation contrast. It strengthens the argument that intact PMC is not routinely required to map familiar spoken words to meaning, while permitting motor assistance with explicit segmentation, classification and particular listening demands. It complements patient dissociations in \citet{stasenko:2015_causal} and \citet{rogalsky:2011_are}.

## Scope and cautions

The measured dependent variable is mainly speed in specific choice tasks following stimulation, not every aspect of acoustic discrimination or ecological conversation; a null change in the semantic task is not an equivalence test for all motor involvement. Brain stimulation may affect connected processing, and selective interference does not reveal the contents of normal auditory experience.

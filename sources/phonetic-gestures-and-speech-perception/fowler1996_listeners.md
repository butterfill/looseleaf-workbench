# \citet{fowler:1996_listeners} — direct-realism reply to auditory-sound accounts

**Source:** Fowler (1996), *Listeners do hear sounds, not tongues*, *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 99. DOI: [10.1121/1.415237](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1121/1.415237). Research ref: papers:fowler:1996_listeners.

## Target and main argument

Despite the title's deliberately paradoxical wording, the paper **defends** direct perception of vocal-tract gestures: listeners certainly hear *sounds*, but what sound specifies and presents is the public articulatory event. It sharply distinguishes the direct-realist thesis from Liberman and Mattingly's specialised perceptual motor-command system (*§I*, pp. 1730–32).

Fowler argues the evidence is convergent across multiple phenomena (§III): cross-modal McGurk integration, shadowing latency, coarticulatory context, parsing and trading relations. Her response to auditory accounts is not that acoustics are unimportant; rather acoustic information is a *specifier* of distal speaking events, much as light specifies visible objects (§IV). Against an animal-based objection, she explicitly entertains birds/non-speaking animals perceiving human vocal actions from their acoustic consequences (§IV.B).

## Philosophical use

Essential adversarial counterpoint for a workbench assessment considering \citet{diehl:1989_objects}: absence of necessary motor simulation is *not* by itself evidence against direct realism, and a demonstration of sensitivity to acoustic patterns alone does not distinguish whether acoustics are perceptual objects or specifying media. A discriminating argument requires comparing common explanatory predictions and not merely counting activated brain regions.

## Cautions

Much of this is conceptual interpretation of historical experiments, not a fresh causal intervention. The inference from ecological specification and cross-modal binding to perceived *gestural content* is contested; learned cue integration can also explain some results. Fowler's distal public gesture should not be conflated with a *speaker's intended motor command*.

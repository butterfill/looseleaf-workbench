# \citet{daube:2019_simple} — acoustic-edge explanations of apparent phoneme codes

**Source:** Daube, Ince and Groß (2019), *Simple Acoustic Features Can Explain Phoneme-Based Predictions of Cortical Responses to Speech*, *Current Biology* 29. DOI: [10.1016/j.cub.2019.04.067](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1016/j.cub.2019.04.067). Research ref: papers:daube:2019_simple.

## What was compared

The paper reanalyses MEG recordings from 24 listeners hearing nearly an hour of continuous narrative, contrasting neural encoding models based on spectrograms, spectrotemporal acoustic edges/derivatives and annotated phoneme/articulatory features. Nested cross-validation and partial-information decomposition separate unique from redundant predictions; findings were checked on a public EEG narrative dataset (*Methods*, *Results*, Figs 2–6).

The acoustic-edge model outperformed simpler spectrograms and could explain the apparent advantages of linguistically annotated feature models. In the published analysis, phoneme-labelled models contributed very little information not already predicted from the better acoustic features; the EEG analysis gave a comparable pattern. The study warns that neural decodability of phoneme labels need not indicate a distinctive high-level phoneme representation when labels correlate with informative sound dynamics.

## Relevance and scope

A concrete *acoustic rival model* to claims that phoneme-feature decoding directly demonstrates gestural or categorical perceptual format. A fair gesture-representation claim must show explanatory benefit beyond strong acoustic-only baselines under matched evaluation.

The result is local to **MEG/EEG responses and the tested feature spaces**—it does not disprove abstract phonemic categories, speech-motor interactions or perception of public articulations. It should also not be read as saying that all phonetic contrasts reduce to low-level acoustics.

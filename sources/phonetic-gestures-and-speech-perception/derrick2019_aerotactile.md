# \citet{derrick:2019_aerotactile} — response format and the limits of aero-tactile enhancement

**Source:** Derrick, Madappallimattam and Theys (2019), *Aero-tactile integration during speech perception: Effect of response and stimulus characteristics on syllable identification*, *The Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* **146**(3), 1605–1614. DOI: [10.1121/1.5125131](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1121/1.5125131). Research ref: papers:derrick:2019_aerotactile.

## Question and experimental contrast

Earlier two-alternative forced-choice (2AFC) experiments such as \citet{gick:2009_aerotactile} found that air puffs paired with speech affect identification of aspirated/voiceless consonants; a subsequent open-response continuous-speech experiment had not found a comparable enhancement. Those studies confounded **response format** (closed vs open) with **stimulus complexity** (isolated syllables vs sentences). The present study asks whether the difference persists when the open-response task also involves *isolated syllables*, and whether place and voicing influence identification thresholds (*Introduction §§I.A–C*, pp. 1605–1607).

**Experiment 1 (open choice):** Forty-four people were recruited, of whom **34** met hearing/language criteria. They heard noisy recordings of /pa/, /ba/, /ka/ and /ga/, with or without a brief (~80-ms) air puff delivered to the suprasternal notch at consonantal release, and **typed whatever syllable they heard**. Eight interleaved QUEST adaptive staircases estimated signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) at about **80% correct** (*Methods §II.A*, pp. 1607–1609). Some staircases of five listeners hit an SNR ceiling and were omitted from the relevant summaries.

**Experiment 2 (closed choice):** A separate **40-person** sample completed a larger audiovisual–tactile experiment; this paper analyses the **four masked/still-video conditions**, to compare auditory speech with or without the air puff. The tested spoken tokens were /pa/ and /ga/, with two response alternatives, and adaptive staircases targeting about **82% correct** (*Methods §II.B*, pp. 1609–1610). **These were different participants and recordings**, as well as different task and stimulus arrangements.

**Outcome measure:** The dependent variable is the SNR required for a target proportion of **correct identification**, not a direct measure of how an articulatory gesture is experienced. *Lower* SNR thresholds indicate better performance in noise.

## Main results

1. **No detectable airflow-congruence benefit in the open-choice experiment.** The selected mixed-effects model did not retain aero-tactile congruence. It did show effects of **place** and a **place × voicing interaction** (Table II, p. 1611). Voiceless labials and voiced velars tended to be identified at lower SNRs than their counterparts. These place/voicing differences must **not** be redescribed as benefits of the tactile cue.
2. **A congruence effect in the closed-choice experiment.** Its selected model included a significant aero-tactile congruence effect (Table IV: \(p = .009\)): matched sound/puff arrangements permitted identification at lower noise-adjusted SNRs than mismatched arrangements (Results §III.B, pp. 1611–1612).
3. **The cross-experiment comparison found a task-by-congruence interaction**, with Table V reporting \(p = .048\). In Fig. 4 the congruence contrast is approximately 3 dB for the closed-choice task and much smaller for open choice. The authors conclude that response demands strongly condition whether the aero-tactile effect is observed (Results §III.C; Discussion, pp. 1612–1614).

## What this establishes for the gestural-perception inquiry

The positive result of \citet{gick:2009_aerotactile} is **not an across-the-board enhancement of speech recognition by externally supplied articulatory consequences**. Providing an airflow cue can affect accuracy thresholds in a constrained choice task without reliably affecting open-ended syllable identification. Thus an inference from the closed-choice effect to *mandatory* incorporation of articulatory information during ordinary understanding would be especially insecure.

The study leaves open at least three explanations: genuinely multisensory integration whose weighting depends on task; explicit response/decision or expectation biases induced by alternatives; and cue-reliability effects when acoustic difficulty differs. A puff at the neck is a **possible external consequence of articulation**, not an observation of the speaker's vocal-tract motion, still less of the speaker's **intended motor command**. Neither the positive nor null finding settles whether gestures are the *objects* or basic units of auditory speech perception.

## Important qualifications and follow-up

- **The response-type interpretation is suggestive, not a clean isolation of response format.** The open and closed experiments used **different participants, utterances/recordings, numbers of possible syllables, presentation protocols, staircase criteria (80% vs 82%) and task context**. In the 2AFC comparison, /pa/ vs /ga/ also varies **place and voicing together**. It is therefore too strong to conclude from the interaction alone that response type *causes* the entire difference.
- The cross-experiment interaction is **borderline at \(p=.048\)** (Table V), and the absence of a retained congruence effect in the open-choice model is not a formal equivalence test proving there is *no* such effect.
- The airflow apparatus delivered roughly **one-twelfth of natural speech airflow** despite comparable pressure (*Discussion*, p. 1613); the authors acknowledge that this might matter more for open-response tasks, and that they cannot yet disentangle motor-learning from external-cue accounts. Restricted consonant sets and artificial puffs constrain ecological validity.
- A better discriminating test would vary **response format within the same listeners using acoustically identical tokens and matched air-puff parameters**, measure perceptual sensitivity against criterion shifts, and distinguish gestures actually executed from cues merely correlated with them.

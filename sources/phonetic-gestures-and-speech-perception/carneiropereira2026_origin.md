# \citet{carneiropereira:2026_origin} — what silent articulation changes

**Source:** Carneiro Pereira, Patri and Vannuscorps (2026), *On the Origin of the Effect of Silent Articulation on Speech Perception*, *Scientific Reports*, online version. DOI: [10.1038/s41598-026-63240-5](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1038/s41598-026-63240-5). Research ref: papers:carneiropereira:2026_origin.

## Experiments and findings

The authors examine why silent articulatory movements alter discrimination of speech sounds, particularly when sounds are embedded in noise. **General motor-resource depletion** is one explanation; alternatives involve auditory imagery, phonological effects, specific motor plans and somatosensory signals.

Across three experiments, listeners discriminated a **labial/lingual syllable pair** (approximately /pa/–/ta/) and a **lingual/lingual pair** (/ta/–/ka/), comparing finger movement with two different silent lip movements. Opening and closing the lips selectively worsened performance for the labial-related contrast; **protruding and retracting the lips while keeping them pressed together** did not show the comparable selective effect. The latter movement overlaps in muscular requirements with lip opening, so simple engagement of those motor resources does not suffice to explain the contrast.

The authors interpret the findings as supporting gesture-relevant motor–sensory interactions in listening and as inconsistent with a mere general articulatory-suppression account. Crucially, the finding concerns **effects of the listener's own concurrent mouth configuration and movement** on sound discrimination, not direct observation of a speaker's intended commands.

## What this leaves open

Gesture specificity is philosophically suggestive but underdetermines representational *content*. A perturbation may change predictive auditory or somatosensory information without making gestures themselves objects of perception. Noise, identification/discrimination task demands and the distinction between executed actions and motor intentions remain important for generalisation.

The result should not be summarised as proof that motor simulation is necessary for speech comprehension. It is an intervention showing selective influence under the tested conditions.

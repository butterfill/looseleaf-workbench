# \citet{mcmurray:2022_myth} — categorical perception as an explanandum

**Source:** Bob McMurray (2022), *The Myth of Categorical Perception*, *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 152(6), 3819–3842. DOI: [10.1121/10.0016614](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1121/10.0016614). Research ref: papers:mcmurray:2022_myth.

## Argument and evidence

The review is a deliberately strong critique of the classical use of *categorical perception* (CP). The familiar empirical definition links a steep phoneme-identification function to a characteristic pattern of discrimination. Historically this was taken to indicate that speech is perceived solely as discrete phonetic categories. But identification and discrimination impose very different task demands. Evidence from refined discrimination paradigms, priming, the visual-world paradigm and electrophysiology shows that within-category phonetic detail is preserved and matters for processing.

McMurray argues not merely that a *strong* version of CP is false but that there is little sound positive evidence for the operational construct as traditionally used. In §§III–IV he emphasises continuous encoding, graded categories and flexible use of acoustic cues. §VI analyses how CP assumptions have shaped claims about development, second-language learning and other perceptual domains.

## Consequence for the gestural argument

The inference from phonetic category boundaries to exclusively gestural perceptual objects becomes much weaker. Category-sensitive responses remain real phenomena, but they need not reflect removal of within-category auditory detail, and a task-induced boundary does not uniquely identify the content of perception. This point directly concerns the categorical-perception premise used in §III of \citet{Butterfill:2009vs}.

A useful philosophical caution: even McMurray's criticism cannot establish by itself that gestures are *not* perceived. It undermines a standard *route to proving* that they are perceived. The alternative positive question concerns mechanisms and representations that jointly explain continuous acoustic sensitivity, phonetic categorisation and successful cross-speaker generalisation.

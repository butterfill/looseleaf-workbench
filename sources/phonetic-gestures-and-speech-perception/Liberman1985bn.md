# \citet{Liberman:1985bn} — the revised motor theory

**Source:** Alvin M. Liberman and Ignatius G. Mattingly (1985), *The Motor Theory of Speech Perception Revised*, *Cognition* 21, 1–36. Verified Research ref: papers:Liberman:1985bn.

## What the original argues

The revised theory postulates a specialised phonetic module which detects the speaker's **intended phonic gestures** through acoustic signals. Gestures are not identical to peripheral articulator trajectories: the relevant invariants are more remote structures controlling movement. Gestures underlie phonetic categories, but their acoustic consequences overlap in coarticulated speech. The theory therefore rejects a simple account on which listeners first recover stable, independent acoustic pieces and only subsequently translate them into phonetic categories.

The authors stress the relation between phonetic and ordinary auditory processing, and the particularities of a perceptual module connected to speech production. The thesis that *gestures are perceptual objects* is stronger than the observation that phonetic experience is categorical.

## What it does, and does not, establish for this inquiry

The original is essential for identifying the exact historical explanandum: context-conditioned acoustic variation and phonetic constancy, not just an incidental mirror-like motor response. It provides a theoretically coherent proposed solution. It is not itself evidence that later TMS effects demonstrate perception of intended commands, or that categorical labelling entails a uniquely gestural object.

Its specialised-module assumption should be considered separately from the object-of-perception thesis and from modern sensorimotor interactions. The intended motor structure and the actual articulatory event might come apart, creating an additional contrast between the revised motor theory and more direct-realist theories of public speech actions.

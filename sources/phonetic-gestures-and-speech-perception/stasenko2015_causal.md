# \citet{stasenko:2015_causal} — impaired production, spared discrimination

**Source:** Stasenko, Bonn, Teghipco, Garcea, Sweet, Dombovy, McDonough and Mahon (2015), *A Causal Test of the Motor Theory of Speech Perception: A Case of Impaired Speech Production and Spared Speech Perception*, *Cognitive Neuropsychology* 32(2), 38–57. DOI: [10.1080/02643294.2015.1035702](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1080/02643294.2015.1035702). Research ref: papers:stasenko:2015_causal.

## Case and findings

The authors studied an individual (AD) with a large left frontal lesion and substantial speech-production impairment. Tongue ultrasound supported a motor contribution to the production problems. The patient showed **normal phonetic boundary behaviour in a discrimination task** and largely preserved basic auditory language comprehension, but serious difficulty **identifying or labelling** the speech-sound categories in a related task. A control naming task reduced the plausibility of treating this simply as a general inability to apply labels.

The study thus disaggregates auditory-phonetic discrimination, explicit identification and speech production. Its strongest contribution is not the generic observation that comprehension can be spared after frontal damage, but a within-patient contrast between relatively preserved discrimination and impaired phoneme labelling.

## Interpretation and limits

The result is difficult for a strict motor theory requiring an intact production system to discriminate phonetic contrasts. It fits views in which motor processes support some demanding categorisation, working-memory or explicit response tasks, or provide helpful predictions under degraded conditions. The authors themselves consider modulatory roles and tasks conducted in noise versus ordinary listening.

**Limits:** this is a single case with a complex lesion and concurrent language impairments, not an intervention selectively removing every motor representation. Preserved discrimination does not establish that motor circuits never contribute, and impaired labelling need not be purely motoric.

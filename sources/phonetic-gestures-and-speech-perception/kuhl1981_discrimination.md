# \citet{kuhl:1981_discrimination} — nonhuman sensitivity maxima near phonetic boundaries

**Source:** Kuhl (1981), *Discrimination of speech by nonhuman animals: Basic auditory sensitivities conducive to the perception of speech-sound categories*, *Journal of the Acoustical Society of America* 70. DOI: [10.1121/1.386782](https://libkey.io/libraries/3907/10.1121/1.386782). Research ref: papers:kuhl:1981_discrimination.

## What the psychophysical study adds

Eleven chinchillas underwent same–different testing on VOT stimuli along /da–ta/, using an adaptive staircase to estimate just-noticeable changes. The smallest discrimination thresholds occurred near the previously determined boundary at approximately 33 ms VOT; the direction of VOT change affected sensitivity at adjacent standards (*Methods*, *Results*, Table I, Fig. 4, pp. 340–45).

This advances beyond the identification boundary reported in \citet{kuhl:1975_speech}: it is specifically a nonhuman **sensitivity peak** near a phonetic division. The *Discussion §III.A* carefully distinguishes the 'phoneme-boundary effect' (local maximum of sensitivity) from the strict classical categorical-perception thesis, which expects identification to predict discrimination and within-category detail to be lost.

## Bearing and boundaries

A major early argument that auditory sensitivities can make a contribution to human-like category-boundary effects without obligatory human motor knowledge. Read alongside \citet{mcmurray:2022_myth}; the critical conceptual distinction long predates that review.

Small animal sample, extensive discrimination training and one VOT class. Mammalian auditory constraints can coexist with learned linguistic categories or direct perception of vocal actions: results do not identify the entire representational code.

# \citet{saito:2019_effects} — what pronunciation-instruction studies actually measure

Saito, Kazuya & Luke Plonsky (2019), “Effects of Second Language Pronunciation Teaching Revisited: A Proposed Measurement Framework and Meta-Analysis,” *Language Learning* 69(3):652–708. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1111/lang.12345. Personal Papers: \citet{saito:2019_effects}.

**Access:** Original Personal Papers full text consulted (framework, study selection, outcome coding, results, discussion and conclusion). Online supporting appendices not independently audited.

**Question and conceptual contribution.** The authors classify outcomes along three axes: global versus specific pronunciation; listener or expert judgment versus acoustic measurement; and controlled versus spontaneous production. These differences matter because an intervention can improve a target contrast in monitored word reading without making an entire utterance easier to understand in conversation. Spontaneous production is an imperfect proxy for automatised speech skill, not an independent measure of deliberate articulatory action.

**Evidence.** Reanalysis of 77 published studies (2,573 learners). Mean reported effects of teaching: between-groups d = 0.68 (95% CI [0.49, 0.86]); within-groups d = 0.73 (95% CI [0.69, 0.78]). Strongest and clearest effects concern specific segmental/suprasegmental features in controlled tests. Global and spontaneous effects are less well established, partly because few studies provide the needed combinations of outcomes and study designs.

**Interpretive limits.** This is an outcome-measure framework and quantitative review, not a randomized comparison of articulatory with auditory instruction. The authors detect possible publication bias in funnel plots, restrict the evidence base to published journal studies, and note that some spontaneous subgroups are small. Controlled success may reflect conscious monitoring; it cannot by itself establish procedural mastery, explicit intentions concerning bodily gestures, or altered experiential structure. Their discussion of declarative, procedural and automatised knowledge is theoretically suggestive, rather than a direct experimental dissociation in the reviewed studies.

**Use for the main entry:** Never read “instruction improves pronunciation” without asking *which feature*, *which task* and *which outcome*. Particularly useful for separating production on request from integration into meaning-focused speech.

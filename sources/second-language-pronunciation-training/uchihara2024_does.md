# \citet{uchihara:2024_does} — does perceptual training carry over to production?

Uchihara, Takumi, Michael Karas & Ron I. Thomson (2024), “Does perceptual high variability phonetic training improve L2 speech production? A meta-analysis of perception-production connection,” *Applied Psycholinguistics* 45(4):591–623. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0142716424000195. Personal Papers: \citet{uchihara:2024_does}.

**Access:** Original Personal Papers full text consulted (theoretical framing, HVPT inclusion rules, methods and effects, moderators, discussion, conclusion). Supplemental files and raw analysis not separately audited.

**Design.** Synthesises 31 studies where canonical high-variability *perception* training used multiple talkers, phonetic contexts and corrective feedback, without an inseparable production-training component. There were 43 within-participant and 17 between-participant independent effect sizes.

**Findings.** Production improves, but less robustly than perception: g = 0.49 for pre/post and g = 0.66 for treatment/control contrasts. Mean production gain was about 10.5 percentage points on trained items but 4.5 points on untrained items. Support for retained production improvement was weak; for new items the pooled gain was small and statistically uncertain. At the participant level, the pooled correlation between *gains* in perception and production was negligible (r = .09). The study-level correlation was moderate (r = .45), which could reflect shared study quality rather than within-person causal transfer.

**Clue about instruction.** Studies that provided explicit phonetic information before perceptual training showed larger production gains (g = 0.94) than studies without it (g = 0.45). This moderator is observational across studies: it does not establish an advantage from explicit articulatory information under matched conditions or show that learners acquired gesture concepts. Most tests elicited controlled production; effects depended on elicitation mode and scoring.

**Mechanistic interpretation and its limits.** The paper discusses distinct but coupled perceptual and articulatory learning processes, and the possibility that a learner's own inaccurate speech can supply unhelpful auditory input. It suggests investigating whether production practice should be delayed until perceptual targets are better established. That recommendation is a hypothesis, not a result of a randomized comparison of production-training schedules.

**Use for the main entry:** A particularly valuable dissociation between *hearing a contrast* and *being able to realise it*, including the failure of individual perceptual gain to predict individual motor gain reliably.

# \citet{uchihara:2025_high} — learning perceptual categories from varied speech

Uchihara, Takumi, Michael Karas & Ron I. Thomson (2025), “High variability phonetic training (HVPT): A meta-analysis of L2 perceptual training studies,” *Studies in Second Language Acquisition* 47(3):794–827. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1017/S0272263125100879. Personal Papers: \citet{uchihara:2025_high}.

**Access:** Original Personal Papers full text consulted (HVPT definition, method, primary and moderator analyses, retention/generalisation, discussion). Supplementary materials and coding sheets not independently audited.

**Scope.** Seventy-nine studies of perceptual HVPT using multiple talkers, varied phonetic contexts and immediate feedback. The review addresses improvement, generalisation to untrained examples and retention, not the emergence of new articulatory skills or action concepts.

**Findings.** Mean pre/post perceptual effect g = 0.92 (96 effects), reduced to approximately g = 0.71 by trim-and-fill adjustment for possible publication bias. After influential-case removal, the treatment/control estimate was g = 0.67 (32 effects); the control comparison is more probative than within-group practice effects. Gains remain above baseline at delayed testing (up to six months in the analysed studies), though with some decay, and generalise to new items and talkers with modest losses, especially for unfamiliar talkers. Thus this is evidence of more than memorising the training tokens.

**Theoretical reading.** Training may support perceptual category development, retuned attention to diagnostic cues or more general exemplar distributions. The evidence does not adjudicate among these explanations. Identification training/tests show larger effects than discrimination conditions, but that comparison is entangled with task overlap and other methodological variables. Likewise, canonical HVPT combines talker variability, context variability and corrective feedback; this synthesis does not by itself isolate a necessary ingredient or show that increasing the number of talkers always helps.

**Use for the main entry:** The clearest evidence here is that adults can acquire and generalise novel *perceptual phonetic distinctions*. Perception of an articulatory *gesture as such* is a further claim requiring a different test.

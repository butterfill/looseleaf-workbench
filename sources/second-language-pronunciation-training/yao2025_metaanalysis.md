# \citet{yao:2025_metaanalysis} — what can comparisons of phonetic-training approaches tell us?

Yao, Yao, Mengjie He, Fei Chen & Jiaqiang Zhu (2025), “A Meta-Analysis of Second Language Phonetic Training: Exploring Overall Effect and Moderating Factors,” *Journal of Speech, Language, and Hearing Research* 68(4):1784–1802. DOI: https://doi.org/10.1044/2024_JSLHR-24-00432. Personal Papers: \citet{yao:2025_metaanalysis}.

**Access:** Original Personal Papers full text consulted (eligibility and quality review, comparisons, generalisation, methods, discussion and limitations). Appendices/data not independently reanalysed.

**Scope and estimate.** The review selects 65 controlled studies involving 2,793 L2 learners and reports 223 between-group effect sizes on *segmental* training (unlike some broader pronunciation reviews, which also include suprasegmentals). Overall immediate training effect d = 0.762 (95% CI [0.635, 0.889]).

**Comparisons.** Mean effects vary sharply with both training (perceptual, production, combined) and outcome (identification, discrimination, expert judgment, objective acoustics). Perception outcomes tend to show larger gains than production outcomes. The authors report perceptual training d = 1.119, production training d = 0.914 and combined training d = 0.102 in pooled cross-study categories. These figures do **not** license a claim that adding production practice is harmful: treatment components, populations, dose and test measures differ among studies; the combined-treatment category does not specify a single standard intervention. Most relevant for the philosophical inquiry is the heterogeneity of the *capabilities being tested*.

**Generalisation and caution.** The review also reports substantial effects on untrained items in a subgroup, but with very high heterogeneity. This broader finding should not be collapsed into the much narrower question in \citet{uchihara:2024_does} of whether *perception-only HVPT* leads to durable production of untrained items. The very large reported high-school moderator reflects a particularly thin study base. The paper's interpretation of publication bias and handling of multiple effect sizes warrant further audit. Some narrative statements about statistical significance are inconsistent with the reported p-values, another reason not to elevate subgroup rankings into firm causal conclusions.

**Use for the main entry:** Training helps, but cross-study comparisons offer limited leverage on the *mechanism* of acquiring novel articulatory skills. Properly matched, direct comparisons remain important.

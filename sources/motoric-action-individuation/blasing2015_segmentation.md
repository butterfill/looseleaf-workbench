# \citet{blasing:2015_segmentation}: dance learning and changed perceived boundaries

**Reference.** Bläsing, Bettina E. (2015), “Segmentation of dance movement: effects of expertise, visual familiarity, motor experience and music,” *Frontiers in Psychology* 5, article 1500. DOI 10.3389/fpsyg.2014.01500. \`blasing:2015_segmentation\` is transcribed from the exact \`citation-key\` field of the Personal Papers record, not conjectured; the legacy BibTeX action did not return an entry.

**Experimental evidence.** Experiment 1 compared 10 professional dancers with 12 physically active non-dancers viewing a novel 92-second contemporary-dance phrase 20 times. Dancers marked fewer meaningful boundaries (larger units) across viewing trials; auditory music differently influenced groups. Experiment 2 tested eight amateur dancers before and after **six weeks learning the very phrase**. Boundaries were reduced post-training for *middle viewing trials* (\`z=-2.240, p=.025\`) and selected individual trials, but **there was no significant overall pre/post difference across all trials** (§“Experiment 2”, Results, PDF page 7). The later Discussion offers a broad learning interpretation, but the results demand this qualification.

**Value for the conjecture.** An individually acquired performance repertoire may be associated with a different grouping of observed movement. The before/after design is closer to causal manipulation than simple athlete/novice correlations.

**Limitations.** Small homogeneous sample, repeated viewing of the same video, mixed result across analysis windows, no visual-only or verbal-conceptual training control, possible changes in attention, music and naming of choreographic patterns. Only *count and location of temporal boundaries* were measured; “one action” individuation is not established. Dance phrases are relatively unlike ordinary reach/grasp actions directed to objects and accessible outcomes.

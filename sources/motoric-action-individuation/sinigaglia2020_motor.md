# \citet{sinigaglia:2020_motor}: skill, observational knowledge and interface alternatives

**Reference.** Sinigaglia, Corrado & Stephen A. Butterfill (2020), “Motor Representation and Knowledge of Skilled Action,” in Ellen Fridland & Carlotta Pavese (eds.), *The Routledge Handbook of Philosophy of Skill and Expertise*, 292–305 (Routledge). DOI 10.4324/9781315180809-29. Exact legacy BibTeX entry \`@incollection{sinigaglia:2020_motor,...}\` returned by Personal Papers.

**Relation to the 2015 paper.** The chapter starts from a focused claim about *knowledge acquired when observing others act*: performing an action skill sometimes improves one's observational accuracy (basketball/volleyball, blindfolded training for abnormal walking patterns, infant reaching/grasping; §§23.2–23.3). It conjectures that the shared element in action performance and observation is *motor representation of the action outcome*, and responds to a challenge about differences in representational direction of fit (§23.4). These skill–knowledge correlations and selective interventions are distinct from the further, currently speculative claim that motor representation partly determines *which things count as individual actions in thought*.

**Important advance on the interface.** §23.5 (“How do motor representations influence knowledge states?”) treats **four candidates**: (i) identity between relevant motor representations and knowledge states, (ii) inference/translation, (iii) associations between motor schemas and executable action concepts (drawing on Mylopoulos & Pacherie), and (iv) motor representations shaping experiences of observed action. It does **not** conclude that experience is uniquely established; §23.6 stresses that further discoveries are needed and that several candidate interfaces may coexist. Thus the 2015 proposal about experiences cannot be treated as the only viable connection between motor representation and action thought.

**For the new question.** The association with an *executable action concept* is especially relevant. It could explain why reaching or grasping concepts are activated by matched motor schemas but still needs a theory of **why those concepts, rather than arbitrary cross-boundary combinations, structure action *individuals***. Association alone might explain facilitation of recognition without individuation. Experience may provide an action index; no studied mechanism yet fixes the privileged motor-conceptual decomposition.

**Unresolved.** The chapter's evidential base is predominantly action *knowledge* (recognizing and predicting goals), not temporal segmentation, concept acquisition, identity tracking or individuation judgements. Attribution of a privileged action repertoire to the motoric would need distinct experiments. The skill to *observe* a movement is not the same as the ability to select the right parts as individual actions.

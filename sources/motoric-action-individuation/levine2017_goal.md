# \citet{levine:2017_goal}: intentional endpoints and expertise in segmentation

**Reference.** Levine, Dani, Kathy Hirsh-Pasek, Amy Pace & Roberta Michnick Golinkoff (2017), “A goal bias in action: The boundaries adults perceive in events align with sites of actor intent,” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: Learning, Memory, and Cognition* 43(6):916–927. DOI 10.1037/xlm0000364. Personal Papers provides the literal \`citation-key\` \`levine:2017_goal\` in its metadata.

**Access.** Personal Papers has bibliographic metadata but its full-text retrieval route did not serve this work. The 12-page original published journal text was consulted via an author-hosted PDF: https://templeinfantlab.com/wp-content/uploads/sites/2/2017/12/A-Goal-Bias-in-Action.pdf. This PDF is not represented as retrieved from the Research full-text endpoint; graphical content/figures were not independently audited.

**Studies.** Study 1 asked skilled informants to mark *sources* and *goals* (beginnings/ends) of meaningful subactions in an Olympic figure-skating routine, with freedom to rewatch. Experts agreed relatively strongly on the timing of goals. Study 2 compared *online* segmentation by skating experts, visually familiarized novices, and unfamiliarized novices. Marked boundaries aligned with sources and goals from the first study, and with goals more than starts; expertise increased this correspondence while short visual familiarization did not produce the same improvement. Study 3 reversed the sequence and obtained unexpectedly persistent correspondence to perceived goals (original article, pp.916–927).

**Why interesting.** An observer's segmentation is biased toward **action endpoints**, not just generic speed/position changes. Motor skill can correlate with alignment to them. Since the theory concerns what is one *goal-directed action*, this is a closer outcome than general recognition performance.

**Unresolved.** Observers may rely on expert conceptual knowledge of skating goals and conventions. Familiarization in Study 2 is much shallower than athletes' experience and cannot equate their semantic expertise. A goal-biased boundary may track the **end of one action** without showing motor representations establish the ontology of those actions.

# \citet{sun:2026_perceiving}: event boundaries within ostensibly single actions

**Reference.** Sun, Zekun & Samuel D. McDougle (2026), “Perceiving event structure in brief actions,” *Cognitive Psychology* 162, 101768. DOI 10.1016/j.cogpsych.2025.101768. The exact \`citation-key\` \`sun:2026_perceiving\` was read from Personal Papers metadata.

**Design.** Seven preregistered experiments used brief motion-capture actions lasting roughly 1–4 seconds (e.g., a kick or a golf swing). Experiment 1 (98 analysed participants) elicited **one preferred internal segmentation point** for each of 20 short clips; Experiments 2–3 tested incidental temporal slow-down detection or spatial-change detection at versus around that point. Further experiments contrasted meaningful actions with distorted (‘spiralized’) versions retaining aspects of low-level motion and point-light variants.

**Findings.** Observers systematically selected an **internal boundary** even though stimuli were deliberately characterized as individual ‘atomic’ actions (Experiment 1 Results); around 40% chose the peak boundary frame or adjacent frames for an action on average. Boundary locations affected incidental change detection, with poorer accuracy at boundaries than neighbouring frames (Experiment 2, analysed \`n=15\`: \`t(14)=4.52,p=.00049\`; Experiment 3 \`n=19\`: \`t(18)=7.41\`, \`p≈7.1 × 10^-7\`). The semantic-vs-distorted motion comparisons support a contribution beyond simple kinematics but are not interventions on motor representations.

**Conceptual significance.** This is a particularly strong warning against the equation **‘one perceptual segment = one action individual’**. People perceive internal events *within* an action that they could still think of as one unitary action. Internal phases such as preparation and completion do not automatically count as two acts. The proposed motoric thesis needs an independent action-identity outcome, perhaps persistence or sameness tracking under occlusion, not boundary marking alone.

**Cautions.** The deliberate instruction in Experiment 1 to choose a division could create a privileged point despite stimuli being treated as single acts. Later incidental tasks show the selected frames have online perceptual effects, though possibly mediated by semantic expectations; sample sizes for the incidental tests are modest after exclusion. No expertise-based or causal motor manipulation was conducted.

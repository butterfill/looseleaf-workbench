# \citet{Hard:2006qm}: hierarchical encoding links observing and performing actions

**Reference.** Hard, Bridgette Martin, Sandra C. Lozano & Barbara Tversky (2006), “Hierarchical Encoding of Behavior: Translating Perception Into Action,” *Journal of Experimental Psychology: General* 135(4):588–608. Verified legacy BibTeX key \`Hard:2006qm\`; DOI 10.1037/0096-3445.135.4.588 (DOI checked against original publication).

**Procedure.** Across three experiments observers watched novel object-assembly sequences, identified meaningful parts at coarse and fine grains, and later assembled the objects themselves. Manipulations included order of coarse and fine segmentation, explicit instructions to attend to hierarchy, and verbal descriptions while watching. The study explicitly concerns how an observed structured sequence is represented in a format useful for *doing* it.

**Results.** Hierarchical segmentation predicted better accuracy and more hierarchically organized subsequent performance (Experiment 1 and general discussion). Crucially the authors developed an **enclosure** measure: the characteristic *lag of coarse boundaries behind corresponding fine ones*. This had greater predictive validity than simple *alignment* (unsigned coincidence of boundaries); in Experiment 1 enclosure, unlike alignment, strongly correlated with spontaneous verbal summaries of fine actions grouped into higher-order acts (\`r(18)=.79\` for enclosure versus a non-significant negative correlation for alignment). Coarse-first segmentation and attending to hierarchical structure promoted encoding and performance; they also report changes in adopting an observed actor's spatial perspective.

**Why unusually relevant.** Unlike merely pressing a segmentation key, the measures extend to *what is represented*, described and subsequently accomplished. They suggest hierarchies of action units can transfer between observation and performance.

**Critical directionality.** Here observation and explicit goal/schema processing come **before** action execution. The result gives direct reason not to presume that action hierarchies in thought must originate in motor representations; conceptual organization may guide subsequent motor learning. This study measures acquired hierarchical encoding and assembly, not necessarily motor-outcome-governed individuation. It may be a better *measurement model* for the proposed experiment than unadorned boundary counts.
